Three options

An organization has three ways to get such a task done.

OPTION 1

By hand

Coders read every response and look up its code.

Coding by hand is slow when a survey or a census brings in responses in the thousands.

OPTION 2

A large AI service

A large model at a service company reads each response and returns a code.

Every response is sent to the company that runs the service.

OPTION 3

A small model of its own

A small model on the office's own computer suggests five codes, and a coder chooses.

The model has to be built first, and building it requires many examples with the right answers.

This session is about option 3 and about where the examples come from.

Take the options one at a time and let the room name the limit of each before you reveal it. Option 2 is what people know from chat assistants. Option 3 is the subject of the session, and its limit sets up the next two parts: why a small model, and why the examples have to be written.

Standard practice

Step 5 · The training run

In one training run the small model goes from 0 to 68 percent of first codes right.

The scores are the mean of three training runs on 1,000 survey-style test answers. One pass reads 35,515 of the 44,058 training texts, and the other texts are held out.

This is the training run of our small model, recorded at 13 points. Press play. Before training the model gives every answer the same code, and no first code is right. After half of one pass over the training texts it is right for 27 percent of the 1,000 test answers. After one pass it is right for half of them, and after two passes for 63 percent. From there the curve is nearly flat, and the score moves by a few points from one pass to the next. The table shows the same for six of the test answers in one run: a code can be right at one point and wrong at the next. After each pass the model is checked on training texts that it does not learn from. Training stops when that check stops improving, and the best pass is saved. The saved models get 68.4 percent on average. The three runs differ only in their random start.

Small or large

We end with the choice between a small and a large model for a task.

6
  6. 6Small or large
The last part is about the choice between a small and a large model. It has an exercise for the room, the checks before relying on a small model, and the requirements of a first attempt.

Small or large

A small model suits a narrow task that repeats many times.

A small model is a good candidate when
  • the answer comes from a fixed list, such as topics or codes;
  • the same task is repeated many times;
  • the data should stay inside the organization;
  • the internet connection is poor.
A large model is the better choice when
  • the answer is new text, such as a summary or a reply;
  • the questions cannot be listed in advance.

On survey-style answers about occupation, our small model scored about the same as the best of three large models and answered in 0.05 seconds on an ordinary processor. On report topics, a second small model also scored about the same as three large models.

These criteria summarize everything so far. Go back to the survey response from the start: a fixed list of codes, a task that repeats, confidential records. Then reveal the other side. A large model remains the right tool for open questions and for writing. Then reveal the two results. The second result is new to the room. The same process built a model that tags reports with topics from a fixed list of 86, and it was tested on 300 real reports. The annex has its two slides. The description of this session says that a specialized model can be more accurate, more efficient, and cheaper than a general-purpose model. Our two tests support the efficiency. On accuracy, both small models scored about the same as the large models, and neither scored clearly higher. The occupation test has a weakness, because a large model wrote its answers. A test on real cases settles the question for each task.
