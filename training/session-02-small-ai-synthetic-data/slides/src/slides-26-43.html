Tagging reports with topics

An organization wants each report tagged with topics from a fixed list of 86.

The three options again
  • By handTagging by hand is slow.
  • A large AI serviceEvery report would be sent to the company that runs the service.
  • A small model of its ownThe organization has no tagged reports from which to train one.

The report and its topics come from the World Bank documents database.

Step 2 · The small model learns

A simple model gets more reports right as it reads more examples.

This demonstration model runs in the page and counts words only. It stops improving after about 20 examples per topic. The report-tagging model learns all 86 topics from about 20,000 examples.

This model is trained live in the page, on four topics. Start with one example per topic and show the words linked to each topic. Move to ten, then forty, and watch the squares fill. Then type a sentence from the room and see which topic the model gives it.

The original form

The student learns to match the teacher's probability for every possible answer. The probabilities carry more information than the teacher's top answer alone.

In this session the teacher also writes the examples.

Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean (2015), "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network." The teacher's memory is that of the 8-bit version used for this model.

Name the method. The teacher is about 190 times the size of the student by number of parameters. In the original form of distillation the student copies the teacher's full set of probabilities. In our case the teacher has a second job, because it writes the training sentences as well.

Step 3 · The small model at work

The student scores every topic and assigns the topics above a threshold.

The student assigns a topic when its score is at least 0.03, up to five topics per report. The report and the scores are recorded.

Here is the student at work on the report we saw earlier. It gives each of the 86 topics a score, and the chart shows the top eight. Four topics pass the threshold, and all four are among the topics recorded for the report. One report proves little, so the next slide gives the result on 300 reports.

Results on 300 real reports

The student scored about the same as three large models asked directly.

F1 on 300 real report abstracts, on a scale from 0 to 1. The student's scores are the mean of three training runs.

Show the three large models first. Each one was asked directly for the topics of every report. Then reveal the student after the first set of sentences, and after the further rounds on its weak topics. The gap closes from about 0.06 to less than 0.01.

Speed and computer

The student answers in 0.19 seconds on an ordinary processor.

The times of the hosted services include the network and the service's queue. The CPU time was measured with four threads of a server processor on 100 of the abstracts, and the time of Qwen3.8-27B on 50.

The axis is logarithmic, so each step to the right is ten times slower. The student on an ordinary processor is about 3 to 20 times faster than the large models, and those ran on a GPU or through an online service. This is the practical gain: the model fits on a laptop and needs no connection.

Cost of building

Each model was built in less than an hour on one server.

Requests, tokens and times come from the logs of the runs. Open models wrote and checked the examples on one server with two GPUs, so no price for each token was paid. The price is an example for comparison with the current price list of a provider. The time of the people who wrote the requests and reviewed the examples was not recorded.

People ask what this costs, so here is what we measured. For report topics, the large model answered 1,750 requests and the whole build took 32 minutes, with six training runs of the small model inside that time. The two occupation builds are larger because a second model checks every example. Now select a price. We ran open models on our own server, so we paid no price for each token. With a hosted service, the cost is the number of tokens times the provider's price, and prices change often. Select the three examples to show the range. We did not measure our own time for writing the requests, reading samples, and deciding what to fix.

Small or large

A small model suits a narrow task that repeats many times.

A small model is a good candidate when
  • the answer comes from a fixed list, such as topics or codes;
  • the same task is repeated many times;
  • the data should stay inside the organization;
  • the internet connection is poor.
A large model is the better choice when
  • the answer is new text, such as a summary or a reply;
  • the questions cannot be listed in advance.

On report topics, our small model scored about the same as three large models and answered faster on an ordinary processor. On occupation codes, it scored lower with written titles alone and higher once labelled job titles were added.

These criteria summarize everything so far. Go back to the survey response from the start: a fixed list of codes, a task that repeats, confidential records. Then reveal the other side. A large model remains the right tool for open questions and for writing. Then reveal the two results. The description of this session says that a specialized model can be more accurate, more efficient, and cheaper than a general-purpose model. Our two tests support the efficiency. On accuracy, the small model reached the large models on report topics. On occupation codes, the first code was right for 35 of 100 real job titles when the model had learned from written titles alone, and for 50 of 100 with the best large model. With labelled job titles and their rewritten answers added, the small model reached 60 of 100. Say that this last test favours the small model, because its labelled titles come from the same job board as the test titles. A test on real cases settles the question for each task.

Before relying on a small model

A team checks four points before it relies on a small model.

  1. 1The model was tested on real documents from the place of use, with known answers.
  2. 2The test covers the topics or codes that matter.
  3. 3The score was compared with a large model on the same test.
  4. 4A person reviews a sample of the output at regular intervals.
This is the slide to take back to the office. Each point answers one of the limits we saw. If a supplier offers a small model, these are the four questions to ask.

Here is what you need for a first attempt on your own task. Start with the people. The specialist matters most, because the specialist knows where two groups are easy to confuse and can tell a realistic example from a poor one. Then the real cases. We used 788 real reports for report tagging, and the occupation model was tested on 1,000 real job titles. Collect them first and keep them away from training. Then the computer. Each training run took between one and four minutes on one server GPU, and a smaller GPU takes longer.
