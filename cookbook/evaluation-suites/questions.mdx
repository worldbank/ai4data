# 2. Questions and labels

**Question:** Where do test questions and labels come from?

## Rationale

A suite is as good as its questions. Questions staff invent reflect what
staff think users ask; questions from search logs, help-desk tickets, and
failed answers reflect what users ask. Labels (the expected series, the
expected value, the expected refusal) are decisions, and two people
making them independently disagree more often than expected, mostly on
the edges: a near concept, a question with no answer, a series that
exists in two forms. The disagreements are where the labelling guide is
written, and the agreement between labellers is the ceiling for every
score the suite will produce.

## Target state

- Question sets are built from real queries and recorded failures, with paraphrases, near concepts, and questions with no answer, in every supported language.
- A labelling guide states the rules; two people label independently; their agreement is measured and the disagreements are adjudicated and fed back into the guide.
- Sets are versioned with a held-out part, and every question records who added it and from where.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Build a first set of fifty questions with expected answers, including ten with no answer; version it. |
| **AI-ready** | Add real queries and failures each month; write the labelling guide; label with two people and measure agreement; cover every language with native speakers; keep a held-out part. |
| **AI-native** | Sample production traffic into the set on a schedule ([chapter 8](./production.mdx)); maintain the guide from disagreements; share the set format with other organizations. |

:::info[Running example]
Twenty search questions labelled by two people: seventeen agree, three
differ, and all three disagreements involve whether a near concept has
an answer (infant mortality when only under-five mortality exists). The
agreement is 0.85 and Cohen's kappa 0.82, and the labelling guide gains a
rule and an example.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="2.1 Build the set from real queries and failures" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination staff; speakers of each supported language" time="One day for the first fifty">

**Result:** the organization has a file of test questions with their
expected answers, built from what users ask and kept under version
control.

A test question is a question with a known correct answer: for a
search, the series that should come first; for an assistant, the value
it should state, or the fact that it should decline. The best questions
come from real users: search logs, help-desk tickets, press enquiries,
and the answers that went wrong. Questions staff invent tend to ask
what the system can answer.

The file follows the format of the dissemination cookbook's
[question set](pathname:///cookbook-files/ai-ready-dissemination/eval_questions.csv).
Each row is one question. The columns are:

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `question`, `language` | The question text and its language |
| `expected_id` | The identifier of the series a specialist would point to, or `NONE` when the organization has no matching series |
| `expected_value`, `expected_behaviour` | The value an assistant should give and what it should do (answer, say the data are not published, refuse) |
| `phrasing` | Whether the question uses the official title or a paraphrase |
| `added_by`, `source` | Who added the question and from where |

1. Collect questions from four sources: search logs (queries that
   returned nothing, or whose results nobody clicked); help-desk and
   press questions; the monthly answer review; and the failures
   recorded by every other test suite. Mark each question with its
   source.
2. Add the questions users do not type but the suite needs: near
   concepts the organization does not publish (expected answer `NONE`),
   questions in every supported language written by native speakers,
   and questions that must be refused (confidential data, individuals).
3. Save the file under version control and record who added each
   question.
4. Set aside a fifth of the questions, chosen at random once, as a
   held-out part that nobody uses while developing the system. It
   shows how the system does on questions it was never tuned on.

**Worked example.** The first fifty questions of the example
organization come from four places. Twenty-two are search queries from
the last quarter that returned nothing or whose results nobody clicked,
such as "jobless rate" and "how many kids finish primary". Nine are
help-desk questions, such as "Is an inflation rate published?", which
gets the expected identifier `NONE`. Eight are failed answers from the monthly
review, with the expected value filled in. Eleven were written by the
French-speaking statistician for coverage. Ten of the fifty are drawn
at random into the held-out list, and the file records each question's
source and the initials of the person who added it.

**What this does not do.** A set built from one period's logs reflects
that period's users and events. The set grows every month and is never
finished. A question translated by machine is a placeholder until a
native speaker has reviewed it, and the file says which ones those are.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="2.2 Write the labelling guide and label with two people" level="AI-ready" skills="Dissemination staff; two labellers" time="One day for the guide; an hour per fifty questions per labeller">

**Result:** the organization has a written guide that says how to
decide the expected answer of a question, two sets of labels produced
independently from it, and a measure of how often the two labellers
agreed.

Deciding the expected answer of a question (its label) sounds easy and
is not. Two people who label the same questions independently disagree
more often than expected, mostly at the edges: is "infant mortality" a
question about under-five mortality, or a question with no answer? The
disagreements are the material from which the labelling guide is
written, and the rate of agreement between the two people is the
ceiling for every score the suite will ever produce: a system cannot be
measured more precisely than its labels.

The example file has twenty questions labelled by two people (columns
`label_a` and `label_b`). The script below reports how often they
agreed, the agreement corrected for chance (Cohen's kappa, which is 1
for perfect agreement and 0 for agreement no better than guessing), and
the questions they disagreed on:

```python file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/evaluation-suites/label_agreement.py
```

```bash
python label_agreement.py labels_two_annotators.csv
```

Output for the running example:

```text
20 items: agreement 0.85 (17/20), Cohen's kappa 0.82

disagreements to adjudicate:
  q04: 'infant mortality': A NONE / B HE_U5_MORT_RATE
  q07: 'primary school attendance': A ED_PRIMARY_NER_PCT / B NONE
  q14: 'labour force size': A NONE / B LF_UNEMP_PCT

3 of 3 disagreements involve NONE: the guide's rule on near concepts needs an example
```

The first line gives the raw agreement and the kappa. The list names
each question the two labellers decided differently, with both labels.
The last line says what the disagreements have in common, which is
where the guide needs work.

1. Write the guide, with an example for each rule. It says:
   - what counts as the expected series (the one a specialist would
     point to);
   - when the answer is `NONE` (no published series covers the concept,
     including near concepts);
   - what to do when several series could answer (choose the most
     specific published one).
2. Have two people label the questions independently, each with the
   guide. Run the script and read the agreement, the kappa, and the
   number of questions.
3. Settle each disagreement with a third person or in a meeting. Record
   the decision and add the case to the guide as an example. In the
   running example every disagreement is about near concepts, so that
   is the rule to sharpen.
4. After the guide changes, label a fresh sample. Agreement should
   rise.

**Worked example.** The three disagreements above are settled in a
twenty-minute meeting with the health and education statisticians. For
"infant mortality", the decision is `NONE`, because infant mortality
and under-five mortality are different indicators and the organization
publishes only the second. For "primary school attendance", the
decision is the net enrolment rate, because attendance and enrolment
are close enough that a user asking one wants the other, with a note in
the answer. For "labour force size", the decision is `NONE`, because
the unemployment rate does not give the size of the labour force. The
guide gains the rule "a near concept is `NONE` when the published series
measures a different population or event, and the nearest series when it
measures the same thing by a different name", with the three cases as
examples.

**What this does not do.** Agreement measures whether two people are
consistent with each other. Two consistent people can share the same
wrong idea about a series. The specialist who owns each series reads
its labels once; after that, the guide carries that knowledge.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="2.3 Keep the set versioned, sliced, and held out" level="AI-ready" skills="Developer" time="Half a day to set up">

**Result:** every test run records which version of the question set it
used, every question carries the groups it belongs to, and a held-out
part exists that development never touches.

A slice is a group of questions scored on its own: the French
questions, the paraphrased questions, the questions with no answer.
Scoring by slice is what shows that a change helped one group and
broke another.

1. Keep the set under version control with a short changelog. Every
   test run records the set's version with its scores, so that a change
   in the score can be traced to a change in the set or in the system.
2. Give every question its slice fields: language; official title or
   paraphrase; question type (a value, a definition, a trend, a
   comparison); and whether it has no answer. The scoring scripts of the
   later chapters group by these fields.
3. Keep the held-out fifth in a separate list. Development runs on the
   rest, and the comparison of [chapter 7](./gates.mdx) reports the
   held-out part on its own.
4. Once the held-out part has been used to decide several changes, it
   has been seen too often. Replace it with a fresh fifth drawn from the
   questions added since.

**Worked example.** The set's changelog reads, for version 1.2: "added
14 questions from the October production review; adjudicated q04, q07,
q14; held-out list unchanged". Every question carries its language, its
phrasing flag, its type, and whether its expected identifier is `NONE`.
After the held-out list has decided four changes, the developer draws a
fresh ten from the thirty questions added since version 1.0, retires the
old list into the development part, and records the rotation in the
changelog as version 2.0. The comparison of chapter 7 reports the new
held-out list on its own from then on.

**What this does not do.** Versioning keeps runs comparable; it does
not keep the set representative of users. The monthly sample of real
traffic in [chapter 8](./production.mdx) does that.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Sources:** search logs, help-desk and press questions, answer reviews, failures from other suites, production samples.
- **Format:** the CSV of the dissemination cookbook with slice fields and provenance per question; JSON lines for answer suites with expected values and behaviour.
- **Labelling:** a written guide; two labellers; Label Studio or a spreadsheet; the agreement script; adjudication recorded.
- **Versioning:** version control with a changelog; a held-out list; the set version recorded with every run.

## Common mistakes

- **Questions written by the people who built the system.** They ask what it can answer; users ask what they need.
- **One labeller.** The errors are invisible and the ceiling unknown.
- **No `NONE` questions.** A system that answers everything scores perfectly and misleads the public.
- **Held-out part reused for years.** It has been seen; rotate it.

## Verification

- **Sources.** Every question has a recorded source, and real queries make up a growing share.
- **Agreement.** Agreement has been measured on the latest labelling round, the disagreements adjudicated, and the guide updated.
- **Versioning.** Every run records the set version, the held-out list exists, and development has not run on it.
