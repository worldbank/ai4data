# 6. Validation of automatic graders

**Question:** How are automatic graders validated?

## Rationale

A rubric applied by readers scales to thirty answers a month; a language
model applied as a grader scales to every answer of every run. The grader is a second system that can be wrong, and it is wrong in
characteristic ways. It passes long, confident answers more often than
readers do. It prefers the first of two answers it is shown. It agrees
with itself across runs while disagreeing with people. None of
this makes graders unusable; it makes validation a requirement. A grader
is validated against human scores on a sample, its biases are measured
and corrected in the rubric, a share of its grades is reviewed every run,
and it is validated again whenever the rubric or the model changes.

## Target state

- Every grader in use has a validation record: agreement and kappa with human scores on at least a hundred items per task, false-pass and false-fail rates, and bias checks.
- The rubric contains the rules that the bias checks required (a length rule, randomized positions).
- A share of grades is reviewed by a person each run, and the grader is re-validated on every rubric or model change.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Use readers for the rubric; no grader. |
| **AI-ready** | Validate a grader on a hundred human-scored items per task; check length and position bias; review a share of grades per run. |
| **AI-native** | Re-validate automatically when the rubric or the model changes; track grader agreement over time; use two graders where they disagree as a signal for review. |

:::info[Running example]
Twenty answers scored by a reader and by a grader. They agree on
fifteen; the grader passes four answers the reader failed and fails one
the reader passed. The bias check shows the grader passing every long
answer while the reader passed six in ten, and the rubric gains a
length rule.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="6.1 Measure agreement with human scores and the bias checks" level="AI-ready" skills="Python; readers for the human scores" time="One day for a hundred items">

**Result:** the organization knows how often a language model used as
a grader agrees with human readers, how often it passes answers the
readers failed, and whether it favours long answers or a particular
position.

A grader is a language model that applies the rubric of
[chapter 4](./answers.mdx) to answers in place of a human reader. It
can score every answer of every run, and it can be wrong in ways
readers are not: graders tend to pass long, confident answers, and to
prefer the first of two answers shown to them. Before a grader's
judgements count for anything, they are compared with readers'
judgements on the same answers.

The example file has twenty answers scored by a reader and by a grader
(1 for pass, 0 for fail), with the length of each answer in words and
the position in which it was shown. The script reports the agreement,
the kappa (agreement corrected for chance), the false passes and false
fails, and the pass rates of reader and grader for short and long
answers and for each position:

```python file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/evaluation-suites/judge_agreement.py
```

```bash
python judge_agreement.py judge_vs_human.csv
```

Output for the running example:

```text
20 items: agreement 0.75, kappa 0.44; grader passes what humans fail 4, fails what humans pass 1

group                      n  human pass  grader pass
short answers             10        0.60         0.50
long answers              10        0.60         1.00
position A                10        0.50         0.70
position B                10        0.70         0.80

length bias: the grader passes long answers 0.50 more often than humans do, relative to short ones; add a length rule to the rubric

The grader is bounded by the humans' own agreement; validate on a hundred items per task and after every rubric or model change.
```

The first line gives the raw agreement, the kappa, and the two kinds
of disagreement. The table compares the pass rates of the reader and
the grader on short and long answers and on each position. The length
line states the bias in one sentence and the rule it calls for.

1. Have readers score a hundred answers per task with the rubric. Have
   two readers score part of them, so that the readers' own agreement is
   known ([chapter 2](./questions.mdx)).
2. Run the grader on the same answers with the same rubric, then run the
   script.
3. Read the false passes first: a grader that passes what readers fail
   lets errors through. Then read the groups: a grader that passes long
   answers more than readers do needs a rule in its instructions that
   length is not evidence; a difference by position means the order of
   answers has to be randomized.
4. Fix the rubric or the grader's instructions, run again, and keep the
   result with the grader's version.

**Worked example.** In the run above, the grader agrees with the reader
on 15 of 20 answers, and four of the five disagreements are answers the
reader failed and the grader passed. All four are long answers. The
table confirms it: the reader passed six of ten long answers and the
grader passed all ten, while on short answers the two nearly agree. The developer adds the sentence "The length and the confidence of an
answer are not evidence that it is correct; judge only the rubric
questions" to the grader's instructions. On a second run of the twenty
answers, the grader passes seven of the ten long answers, and the
agreement rises to 0.90. The result is filed with the grader's version 1.1.

**What this does not do.** A grader cannot agree with readers more than
readers agree with each other. A grader that matches a reader 85 times
in 100, when two readers match each other 85 times in 100, is as good
as a reader and no better.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="6.2 Write the grader's rubric and prompt to resist its biases" level="AI-ready" skills="Developer; subject-matter staff" time="Half a day">

**Result:** the grader has instructions written so that it answers each
rubric question separately with a reason, and so that the biases
measured in recipe 6.1 are countered.

1. Give the grader the question, the records that were retrieved, and
   the answer. Ask it to answer each rubric question with yes or no and
   one line of reason, in a fixed data format. Never ask for a single
   overall score.
2. State the rules the measurements called for. At minimum:
   - length is not evidence;
   - a number counts as right only if it appears in the retrieved
     records;
   - a caveat that the metadata carries and the answer omits is a fail;
   - the order in which answers are shown means nothing.
3. Where answers are compared, randomize their order. Where possible,
   grade each answer on its own.
4. Keep the instructions and the rubric under version control. Any
   change triggers recipe 6.3.

**Worked example.** The grader's prompt for the example organization
gives the question, the retrieved records with their values and
metadata, and the answer. It then asks five questions, one per rubric
item, and requires a JSON object with a yes-or-no field and a one-line
reason for each. The rules section reads: "Length and confidence are not
evidence. A number is correct only if it appears in the retrieved
values. If the metadata marks the series as provisional or broken and
the answer does not say so, answer no to the caveat question. Judge this
answer on its own." When two answers are compared for a different
purpose, the harness shuffles their order on every call. The prompt is
stored as version 1.1 beside the rubric.

**What this does not do.** The instructions reduce the biases that were
measured. A new model brings new biases, which is why the measurement
is repeated.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="6.3 Review a share each run and re-validate on change" level="AI-ready" skills="Readers; developer" time="An hour per run">

**Result:** on every run, a person re-reads a share of the grader's
judgements and the agreement on that share is recorded. After any change
to the rubric or the grader, the full comparison of recipe 6.1 runs
again before the grader is used.

1. On each run, have a reader score a random fifth of the graded
   answers, and all of the failed ones when they are few. Record the
   agreement on that share with the run.
2. When that agreement falls below the level measured in recipe 6.1,
   stop using the grader's judgements for decisions until it has been
   measured again.
3. After any change to the rubric, the instructions, or the model
   behind the grader, run recipe 6.1 again before the grader grades a
   run that decides anything.
4. Where two graders are available, treat their disagreement as a
   signal to send the answer to a reader. Averaging two graders hides
   the disagreement.

**Worked example.** In the November run the grader scores 150 answers.
The reader re-reads 30 drawn at random and all 12 that the grader
failed, and agrees with the grader on 27 of the 30 and 11 of the 12,
which is in line with the validation. In January the provider updates
the model behind the grader. The scheduled comparison of chapter 8 shows
no change in the assistant, but the re-read share drops to 22 of 30. The
grader's judgements are set aside, the hundred-item validation is run
again on the new model, a new length bias is found and countered, and
the grader returns to use at version 2.0.

**What this does not do.** The re-read share catches a grader that
drifts. It does not catch a failure the rubric does not name; those
appear in the production review of [chapter 8](./production.mdx).

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Validation:** the script in this chapter; a hundred items per task; readers' own agreement measured.
- **Bias checks:** length, position, verbosity, self-preference where the grader and the graded system share a model; randomized order.
- **Prompting:** per-question structured output with reasons; rules against the measured biases; versioned.
- **Operation:** a reviewed share per run; re-validation on change; disagreement between graders as a review signal.

## Common mistakes

- **A grader trusted because it is consistent.** Consistent with itself; validate against people.
- **One overall score.** Per-question grades with reasons are what readers can check.
- **Validation done once.** The model behind the grader changes; validate again.
- **Averaging two graders.** Their disagreement is the signal; use it for review.

## Verification

- **Record.** Every grader in use has a validation record with agreement, kappa, false passes, and bias checks, with the grader's version.
- **Rubric.** The rubric contains the rules the checks required, and the order of compared answers is randomized.
- **Operation.** Each run has a reviewed share with its agreement, and re-validation ran after the last change.
