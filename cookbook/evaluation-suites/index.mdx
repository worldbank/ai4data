# Practical Guide to Evaluation Suites for Statistical AI

**A cookbook for teams that deploy AI components in national statistical organizations.** Version 0.1 (working draft), October 2026.

:::note[Working draft]
This guide is a working draft from the AI for Data – Data for AI program. It
collects practical approaches and open standards. It does not
represent official World Bank Group guidance or policy and is not legal
advice; an organization applies it under its own statistical law, data
protection rules, and institutional policies.
Comments and corrections are welcome as
[GitHub issues](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/issues), and the
[contributing](./contributing.md) page explains how to propose a recipe.
:::

Every AI component an organization deploys makes decisions on its behalf:
which series a search returns, what number an assistant states, which
spans an extractor keeps, which draft a curator sees. The organization is
responsible for those decisions, and the only way to know whether a
component makes them well enough, and whether a change made it better or
worse, is an evaluation suite: a set of questions with known answers, run
the same way every time, with measures that mean something and a rule
for what passes. The other cookbooks each describe the suite for their own
component. This guide is about the suites themselves: how they are built,
labelled, measured, validated, used as gates, kept alive in production,
and reported.

This guide is organized around nine questions. The first two are about
what goes into a suite: what should be evaluated and in what order, and
where test questions and labels come from. The next four are about
measurement: how search and retrieval are measured, how generated
answers are measured, how extraction and classification are measured,
and how automatic graders are validated. The last three are about using
the suite: how it gates changes, how a deployed component is evaluated
over time, and how results are reported and compared. Each chapter
answers one question with recipes, maturity levels, worked examples,
and a checklist.

The recipes build on the evaluation practice of the program's workstreams
([Data Discoverability](/docs/data-discoverability/) and the
[PI-FT toolkit](/pift-toolkit/pipeline) for retrieval,
[Monitoring of Data Use](/docs/data_use/) for extraction,
[Proof-Carrying Numbers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06902) for answers)
and on the measures of information retrieval and classification. The
[standards page](./standards.md) lists each and where it applies.

:::tip[The suite is the product's memory]
A component without a suite can be changed and nobody can say whether it
got worse. The suite, versioned with the questions people asked and the
errors found, is what turns a demonstration into a service the
organization can maintain.
:::

## Prerequisites

The recipes assume the following are in place or can be arranged:

- At least one AI component in use or planned (a search, an assistant, an extractor, a drafting tool) and the people who own it.
- Dissemination staff who can write questions and label answers, speakers of each supported language, and a person who can run Python scripts.
- For the AI-ready level, a developer to run suites in continuous integration; for graders, access to a language model under the AI-use policy.

## First steps

For a team with limited time, this sequence gives the most useful result first:

1. List the components and set the required score for the most important one, following [chapter 1](./inventory.mdx). This takes half a day.
2. Build a set of fifty questions from real queries, including ten with no answer, and version it, following [chapter 2](./questions.mdx). This takes a day.
3. Score the component with the chapter that fits it, which is [chapter 3](./retrieval.mdx) for search, [4](./answers.mdx) for answers, or [5](./extraction.mdx) for extraction, and keep the scores with the versions. This takes half a day.
4. Run the suite before and after the next change and compare the runs, as [chapter 7](./gates.mdx) describes.

Graders ([chapter 6](./judges.mdx)), production sampling ([chapter 8](./production.mdx)), and report cards ([chapter 9](./reporting.mdx)) come once the suite gates at least one change.

## Self-assessment

The [self-assessment](./start-here.mdx) takes about five minutes and places
a team's evaluation practice at a level in each chapter.

## Maturity levels

Each chapter describes three levels. A team can sit at different levels in
different chapters, and the first level is a complete, useful state on its
own.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Each component has a question set with known answers, scored by a script, with the scores kept per version. |
| **AI-ready** | Sets are built from real queries, labelled by two people with measured agreement, sliced by language and type, with automatic graders validated and changes gated on paired comparisons with intervals. |
| **AI-native** | Production traffic is sampled into the sets, drift is monitored, report cards are published with every release, and sets are shared in a common format. |

## Running example

The example organization of the dissemination cookbook runs a search, an
assistant, and a mention extractor. Twenty search questions labelled by
two people, two retrieval runs, eight assistant answers, twelve aligned
extraction spans, twenty grader scores, two runs of thirty questions for a
gate, and a month of twenty-four logged queries carry the recipes. All
questions, answers, and scores are invented.

## Scope of the examples

The examples are small on purpose: twenty to thirty items per file, so
that a reader can run them in an afternoon and see the shape of each
measure, and see how wide the intervals are at that size. They are
starting points. Each recipe ends with a **What this does not do** note
that says where the example stops and what the full version adds.
