# 5. Access control, quotas, and terms

**Question:** Who may call the interface, how much, and under which terms?

## Rationale

Public statistics are free to use, and an agent interface over them can be
open to anyone. Open does not have to mean unlimited. One misconfigured
agent can send a thousand requests a minute and take the service down for
everyone else. A leaked key can be abused for months before anyone
notices. And even for free data the organization wants attribution, and a
way to reach whoever runs a client that is sending heavy traffic.

The usual answer is a small number of access tiers. Anonymous clients get
tight limits and nothing else. Registered clients get a key, higher
quotas, and a contact person, in exchange for accepting the terms of use.
Where a product requires the user to be identified, for example a licensed
product or a personal account, the protocol's OAuth-based authorization
identifies the user. Quotas protect the service, the terms protect
attribution, and the client registry and the request log together make
both of them auditable.

## Target state

- The access tiers (public, registered, partner, and user-level) are defined with their limits, their quotas, and their terms, and the AI-use policy records them.
- Each registered client holds a key or a token, has accepted the terms, and has a contact person. A registry holds the records.
- The server, or a gateway in front of it, enforces the per-minute limits and the daily quotas of each tier and returns errors the model can act on.
- A periodic audit compares the request log with the registry and reports unknown clients, clients over their limits, and clients that never accepted the terms.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Public data are served anonymously with a per-client rate limit, and the terms of use for agents are published. |
| **AI-ready** | Registration issues keys. Tiers have quotas. A client registry holds contacts and accepted terms, and the audit of this chapter runs on a schedule. |
| **AI-native** | The protocol's authorization (OAuth 2.1 with protected resource metadata) is in place for user-level access. Tools are scoped per tier, and usage per client is reported to the monitoring. |

:::info[Running example]
The example server has a public tier with a limit of 30 requests a minute
and no key, a registered tier, and a partner tier. One day's request log
shows an anonymous burst that the limit cut off, two requests with a key
the registry does not know, and a registered client that never accepted
the terms. The audit reports all three.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="5.1 Define the tiers, the quotas, and the terms" level="Foundational" skills="Dissemination lead; legal; developer" time="One day">

**Result:** the organization has a short table of access tiers with their
limits and conditions, and a terms-of-use text for agents. Both are
published on the developer page.

A tier is a class of client with its own limits and obligations. Defining
the tiers before the server exists settles two questions at once: how much
load the organization is prepared to serve, and what it asks of the
clients in return. The terms of use for agents are separate from the data
licence. The licence says what may be done with the data; the terms say
what may be done with the service.

1. Define the tiers. The public tier is anonymous, with a low per-minute
   limit and a small daily quota. The registered tier requires a key,
   accepted terms, and a contact, and gets higher limits. The partner
   tier rests on an agreement and gets the highest limits. A user-level
   tier exists only where a product requires it, through the
   authorization of recipe 5.3.
2. Write the terms for agents. They ask for attribution in the form of
   [chapter 4](./provenance.mdx), forbid attempts at re-identification
   and resale of the service, require respect of the limits, and give the
   contact for heavy use. For the data themselves they point to the data
   licence, which is CC BY 4.0 by default.
3. Record the tiers and the terms in the AI-use policy and in the
   component register of the dissemination cookbook, with the name of the
   person who owns the registry.
4. Set the limits from the API's own capacity, because the server passes
   every request upstream. The upstream limit divided by the expected
   number of clients is the starting point, and the audit of recipe 5.4
   shows whether it was right.

**Worked example.** The example organization's table has three rows. The
public tier allows 30 requests a minute and 2,000 a day. The registered
tier allows between 60 and 120 requests a minute, depending on the client,
and 20,000 a day. The partner tier allows 600 a minute under an agreement.
Its terms text asks every client to cite the organization in the form
given in the guidance resource and to contact the data team before
exceeding 20,000 requests a day. The API behind the server handles about
1,500 requests a minute, so the limits leave room for a few dozen
registered clients at once.

**What this does not do.** The terms state expectations. The limits enforce
only the ones the server can see. Attribution is verified by reading
answers, which is the work of [chapter 7](./evaluate.mdx) and of the
[monitoring cookbook](/cookbook/monitoring-data-use/).

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="5.2 Register clients and enforce limits per tier" level="AI-ready" skills="Developer; operations" time="Two days">

**Result:** the organization has a registry of known clients, each with a
key issued at registration, and a server, or a gateway in front of it,
that enforces each tier's limits and returns errors an assistant can act
on.

A key is a secret string the client sends with every request. The server
uses it to look up the client's tier and to count the client's requests.
Keys identify clients, which are programs; they say nothing about the
people using those programs. The registry is the list of clients the
organization knows, and it is kept as a CSV file with one row per client:

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `client_id` | The key prefix, or `pub-anon` for anonymous public clients. |
| `name`, `contact` | Who the client is and how to reach them. |
| `tier` | `public`, `registered`, or `partner`. |
| `requests_per_minute`, `daily_quota` | The limits of the tier. |
| `terms_accepted` | The date the terms were accepted, left empty if they were not. |

The registry of the running example:

```csv file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/serving-statistics-to-agents/client_registry.csv
```

1. Build a registration form that collects the client's name, its
   purpose, its contact, and the acceptance of the terms, and issues a
   key. Store the record in the registry with its tier.
2. Enforce the per-minute limits and the daily quotas per client, either
   in the server or in a gateway in front of it. When a client exceeds a
   limit, return HTTP status 429 with a `Retry-After` header, and a tool
   execution error that tells the model to wait and retry later. Without
   that message, a model retries at once and makes the burst worse.
3. Carry keys in headers (`Authorization: Bearer`), never in URLs, where
   they end up in logs and referrers. Rotate a key on request, revoke it
   on abuse, and log the client identifier with every request, as
   [chapter 6](./safety.mdx) describes.
4. For the public tier, identify anonymous clients by address or session
   only for the purpose of the limit, and keep nothing else about them.

**Worked example.** The running example's registry has five rows. The row
`k-7f3a` is the Ministry of Planning's dashboard, a registered client with
120 requests a minute, a daily quota of 20,000, terms accepted on 2 June
2026, and a contact address. The row `pub-anon` stands for every anonymous
client, with 30 requests a minute and a quota of 2,000. When the dashboard
sends its 121st request in one minute, the gateway returns 429 with
`Retry-After: 30`, and the tool error reads "rate limit reached; retry
after 30 seconds".

**What this does not do.** Keys identify clients and say nothing about
users. A product that needs to know who the user is, or that serves a
licensed product, needs the authorization flow of recipe 5.3.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="5.3 Add OAuth-based authorization for user-level access" level="AI-native" skills="Developer with identity management experience" time="One to two weeks">

**Result:** the server acts as an OAuth 2.1 resource server, as the
protocol specifies. A client obtains a token for a specific user, and the
server serves what that user may see.

OAuth is the standard by which a program obtains permission to act for a
user without ever seeing the user's password. The user signs in at the
organization's identity provider, the identity provider issues a token
that names the user and what they may do, and the client sends that token
with every request. The Model Context Protocol specifies how an MCP server
takes part in this flow, so that any compliant client can authorize a user
without custom code.

1. Publish protected resource metadata (RFC 9728) at the well-known URL.
   The metadata names the authorization server. Clients discover it from
   the `WWW-Authenticate` header of a 401 response, which also states the
   scopes required.
2. Use the organization's identity provider as the authorization server,
   with OAuth 2.1, PKCE, authorization server metadata, and resource
   indicators, so that tokens are bound to this server and cannot be
   replayed elsewhere. Support client ID metadata documents, and
   pre-register the clients the organization knows.
3. Validate the audience and the scopes of every token. Return 401 for an
   invalid token and 403, with the required scope named, for a token
   that lacks it. Define scopes per tool group: `stats:read` for the
   public tools, and a product scope for licensed data.
4. Keep the public tier anonymous. Authorization applies only where the
   product requires a user.

**Worked example.** The example organization has one licensed product, a
set of regional tables available to subscribing ministries. Its server
defines two scopes: `stats:read`, which every token carries, and
`stats:regional`, which the identity provider grants to users from
subscribing ministries. A request for the regional tables with a token
that lacks `stats:regional` receives a 403 response whose body names the
missing scope. The public series need no token at all.

**What this does not do.** Authorization identifies users and their scopes.
It does not decide whether a licensed product may be served to an agent at
all. That decision belongs to the access policy of the
[microdata cookbook](/cookbook/microdata-documentation/access).

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="5.4 Audit the log against the registry" level="AI-ready" skills="Python; operations" time="Minutes per period">

**Result:** a periodic report lists unknown clients, clients over their
limits, rejected requests, and registered clients that never accepted the
terms.

The request log has one row per request, with the timestamp, the client
identifier, the tool called, and the HTTP status. On its own the log says
what happened. Joined with the registry, it says whether what happened was
allowed. The script joins the two and reports per client:

```python file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/serving-statistics-to-agents/quota_audit.py
```

It runs with the registry and the log as arguments:

```bash
python quota_audit.py client_registry.csv agent_requests.csv
```

For the running example the output is:

```text
27 requests from 6 clients; registry lists 5

client     tier        requests busiest min limit/min rejected  note
pub-anon   public            10           8        30        2  
k-4d0f     partner            5           5       600        0  
k-2b8e     registered         4           4        60        0  terms not accepted
k-7f3a     registered         4           4       120        0  
k-0000     unknown            2           2         -        2  not in the registry
k-9c1d     registered         2           2       120        0  

unknown clients: k-0000 (rejected by the server; check for a leaked or revoked key)
registered without accepted terms: k-2b8e (suspend until the terms are accepted)
tool calls served: get_observations 18, search_series 3, get_series_metadata 1, list_code_values 1
```

The table has one row per client seen in the log. The "busiest min"
column is the highest number of requests the client sent in any one
minute, which is what the per-minute limit applies to. The "rejected"
column counts requests the server refused. The lines after the table are
the findings that need action.

1. Run the audit weekly on the request log. The script's exit status
   fails the run when an unknown key or a client without accepted terms
   made requests, so that a scheduled job raises an alert.
2. Act on each finding. An unknown key is revoked or its registration is
   completed. A client without accepted terms is suspended until it
   accepts them. A client that keeps hitting its limit is contacted about
   a higher tier.
3. Feed the per-client counts to the usage statistics of
   [chapter 9](./operate.mdx) and to the
   [monitoring cookbook](/cookbook/monitoring-data-use/access).

**Worked example.** In the run above, the key `k-0000` made two requests
and is not in the registry. Both requests were rejected by the server, so
no data were served, but the operations officer checks whether the key was
revoked recently or whether a registered client's key was leaked and
altered. The client `k-2b8e` is registered and made four requests without
ever accepting the terms, so it is suspended and its contact is asked to
accept them. The anonymous clients sent 8 requests in their busiest
minute, under the limit of 30, and the two rejections among them came from
a short burst earlier in the day.

**What this does not do.** The audit reads what the server logged. It does
not enforce anything. A gateway in front of the server is where the limits
are applied, and the audit confirms that what the gateway did matches the
registry.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Tiers.** Public, registered, partner, and user-level, with limits derived from the upstream API's capacity.
- **Enforcement.** A token bucket per key inside the server, or a gateway (an API management product) that handles keys, quotas, and 429 responses.
- **Authorization.** OAuth 2.1 as the specification defines it, with the organization's identity provider. The Python SDK supports the server side.
- **Registry and audit.** A CSV file or a database table with the fields of this chapter, and the audit script run on a schedule.

## Resources

- [MCP specification: authorization](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/basic/authorization) defines the OAuth 2.1 flow, protected resource metadata, scopes, and the token requirements for HTTP transports.
- [RFC 9728 OAuth 2.0 Protected Resource Metadata](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9728) and [RFC 8707 Resource Indicators](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8707.html) are the two specifications an MCP server implements for authorization.
- [RFC 6585 (HTTP 429 Too Many Requests)](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6585) defines the status code and the `Retry-After` header used for rate limiting.
- The [client registry](pathname:///cookbook-files/serving-statistics-to-agents/client_registry.csv) and the [quota audit](pathname:///cookbook-files/serving-statistics-to-agents/quota_audit.py) are the running example's registry and audit script.
- The [access chapter of the monitoring cookbook](/cookbook/monitoring-data-use/access) counts machine access from the same log.

## Common mistakes

- **No limit on the public tier.** One agent's retry loop becomes everyone's outage.
- **Keys in URLs.** They end up in logs and referrer headers. Keys travel in headers only.
- **A registry nobody audits.** Revoked keys keep working, and the terms are never accepted by anyone.
- **Authorization for everything.** Public data stay anonymous. Authorization is for the products that need a user.

## Verification

- **Tiers.** The tiers are published with their limits and terms and recorded in the AI-use policy.
- **Enforcement.** A burst over the limit receives a 429 response with `Retry-After`, and a request with an unknown key receives 401.
- **Audit.** The weekly run exists, and each of its findings has a recorded action.
- **Authorization.** Where it is implemented, a token issued for another server is rejected, and a missing scope returns 403 with the scope named.
