# 1. Artifact Characterization and Sampling

**Question:** What must the schema describe, and which artifacts provide credible evidence of its requirements?

## Rationale

An artifact is the item that receives one metadata description, such as a report,
dataset, image, or recording. The artifact class is the type of item the schema
will describe. Schema development can go in the wrong direction
if reviewers and AI models are describing different things. A record for an
entire report serves a different purpose from a record for one chart within it.
Agreeing on what receives one metadata record and how that record will be used
keeps discovery focused on useful information. Text and numbers can both be metadata; what
matters is their role in describing the selected item.

The examples selected for discovery also shape the schema. If they are too
similar, important differences in purpose, language, structure, format, or source
may go unnoticed. Selecting varied examples helps reveal those requirements.

A schema that works on the examples used to build it may still miss information
in other materials. Use a development sample to discover and refine fields, and
reserve a separate holdout sample for testing the schema later. Keeping those
items out of development helps reveal gaps that familiar examples may hide.
Related items can still share terminology and structure, so record whether the
two samples share sources, versions, or components of the same object.

## Target state

- A short characterization statement, written by the practitioner, defines what receives one metadata record, how the record will be used, and what information falls outside the task.
- A fixed list of examples for developing the schema and a separate list reserved for validation record each item's identity, source, and reason for selection.
- Development and holdout lists contain no shared items, and any shared sources are documented.

## Prerequisites

- Access to candidate artifacts and their source references.
- A reviewer who understands the type of material and its intended uses.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | A reviewer agrees on what each record describes and selects examples. |
| **AI-ready** | A table records each item's identifier, source, and development or holdout membership. |
| **AI-native** | Optional AI assistance suggests scope or sampling choices; its inputs and suggestions are recorded and checked by a reviewer. |

## Recipes

<Recipe title="1.1 Define the artifact class and descriptive scope" level="Foundational" skills="Domain reviewer; technical practitioner" time="30–60 minutes for a first statement">

**Result:** A short statement defines what each metadata record will describe and what it should help people do.

Use the filled [characterization worksheet](pathname:///cookbook-files/ai-assisted-metadata-schema-development/artifact_characterization.md)
as a starting point for the statement. It is a teaching example prepared for
this guide.

**Input example.** The [Jordan chart](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4data/data-snapshot/blob/626f3c54ba6d8f6e0ba0795f9e544e24b2650faf/snapshots/refugee/027_Jordan-Emergency-Food-Security-Project_figure_001.png) shows food-expenditure shares.
Its title describes the chart; the bar values are its analytical contents, and
the parent-report title supplies context. Decide which of these belongs in the
metadata task before proposing fields.

1. Define the type of item that receives one record: a whole object or a component within it.
2. State what people should be able to find, understand, or do using the metadata.
3. Decide what information belongs in the description, what remains content, and what supporting context may be used.
4. Record the reviewer and version. Check the statement against a contrasting item before accepting it.

**Worked example.** The teaching statement makes these choices for the chart:

| Question | Decision |
|---|---|
| What receives one record? | The chart, described so people can discover and interpret it. |
| What belongs in its description? | Its title about food-expenditure shares describes the chart's subject. |
| What is excluded? | Reconstructing individual bar values or a complete data series. |
| What context may be used? | The parent report can supply context, such as Jordan, but that evidence must be identified separately from what is visible in the chart. |

**Verification.** Another reviewer can use the statement to explain how the
chart title, bar values, and parent-report information should be handled.

**What this does not do.** The statement sets the scope for discovery; it does
not prescribe the final field list.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.2 Select varied examples for schema development" level="AI-ready" skills="Domain reviewer; technical practitioner" time="1–2 hours for a small accessible collection">

**Result:** A documented development sample includes examples likely to reveal different metadata needs.

A schema built from a few similar items may miss fields needed elsewhere in
the collection. Select examples for the differences they expose; a spreadsheet
is sufficient to record the selection.

**Input example.** The study drew on tables and figures from humanitarian
reports, policy research papers, and project documents. These materials differ
in purpose and presentation, so selecting only one kind could hide metadata
needs present in another.

1. Identify differences that could affect the metadata, such as purpose, language, format, structure, or source.
2. Inspect examples across those differences and select a development sample. Record why each group matters to the intended use.
3. Save the selected items' identifiers or filenames, source references, and collection version so the same sample can be revisited.
4. Note exclusions and poorly represented groups. Keep the selection fixed during development; document any later changes. Reserve other examples for validation in Recipe 1.3.

**Worked example.** The Data Snapshot study selected tables and figures from
three institutional collections to capture differences in reporting context.
Each collection contributed 35 tables and 35 figures. Those choices illustrate
one sampling approach; another use case may need different groups and counts.

**Verification.** A reviewer can locate each selected item and explain which
relevant differences the sample covers and which remain unexamined.

**What this does not do.** Varied examples do not establish statistical
representativeness. There is no universal sample size for this method.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.3 Reserve a holdout sample and check for overlap" level="AI-ready" skills="Technical practitioner; evaluation reviewer" time="15–30 minutes with prepared lists">

**Result:** A separate sample is reserved for validation, with any shared sources documented.

The development sample is used to build the schema. The holdout sample is kept
out of that work and used later to check whether the schema describes additional
items adequately.

**Input.** Use the development list from Recipe 1.2 and the remaining candidate
items. For charts from reports, compare both the chart identifiers and their
parent reports: different charts can still share a source.

1. Select holdout items that reflect the intended use, including relevant variation beyond the development examples.
2. Label each selected item as development or holdout. Keep holdout items out of discovery and field design until validation.
3. Compare the lists and resolve any overlap so each item belongs to only one sample. Check related versions and shared sources too: two charts from one report, for example, may have similar content and presentation.
4. Record what is separate and what remains shared. Testing new items from familiar sources supports a different conclusion from testing items from new sources.

:::note[Worked example from the study]
The Data Snapshot study used separate development and holdout snapshots, with
no snapshot in both samples. The samples did share 65 parent reports, so the
test did not establish performance on a wholly separate set of reports.
:::

**Verification.** No item appears in both samples, and any shared sources are
recorded.

**What this does not do.** Checking overlap does not establish that either
sample covers every metadata need. Once holdout findings are used to revise
the schema, a further independent test requires fresh examples.

</Recipe>

## Common mistakes

- A containing-object description can obscure the individual artifacts that need their own records.
- In the snapshot example, extracted data values can be mistaken for reusable descriptive properties.
- Balanced group counts can be mistaken for statistical representativeness.
- Separate items can still share sources, terminology, and structure.

## Verification

- Another reviewer can apply the scope statement consistently to contrasting items.
- Selected items can be located, and the development sample's coverage and exclusions are documented.
- Development and holdout samples contain no shared items; any shared sources are recorded.
