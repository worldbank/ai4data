# 1. Reading a readiness profile

**Question:** What does the readiness profile say about the organization?

## Rationale

An AI-readiness assessment ends with sixty-nine answers, one per
question, each on a four-level scale from A to D. The answers are the
assessment's substance, and the profile is the summary that lets a
planning team see them at once: an average and a level for each of the
twelve dimensions, an average for each of the two pillars, and one
overall score. The framework is explicit that the output is a map of
where the organization stands and where it needs to go, and that low
scores on frontier dimensions are expected for most organizations.

Reading the profile well means two things. The first is to read the
dimension level with the questions behind it, because a dimension at B
with two questions at A and three at C needs different work from one with
five questions at B. The second is to resist the overall score. The
framework encourages organizations to look at dimension-level scores
directly to identify areas for action, and the planning recipes of this
guide never use the overall score for a decision.

## Target state

- The question-level answers are in one file with a score per question, the dimension, and the assessor's note.
- The profile is computed by script from the file, so that the levels and averages are reproducible.
- The planning team can name, for each dimension, the questions that hold it at its level.
- The profile is dated and tied to the framework version that produced it.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Enter the answers in the score file, compute the profile, and read the dimensions with their questions. |
| **AI-ready** | Keep the score file under version control with the assessor's notes, and compute the profile as the first step of every planning cycle. |
| **AI-native** | Take the answers from the assessment platform's export directly, and publish the profile with the pathway document. |

## Recipes

<Recipe title="1.1 Compute the profile from the question scores" level="Foundational" skills="A planning officer who can run a Python script" time="One hour">

**Result:** the organization has its readiness profile as a table it can
reproduce: for each dimension the number of questions, the average, and
the level; for each pillar the average; and the overall score.

The score file has one row per question. The answers A to D carry the
values 0 to 3, as the framework's scoring annex states. A question that
does not apply, for example because the gateway question placed the
organization outside the AI-maturity questions, is marked NA and left out
of the averages.

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `question_id`, `dimension`, `pillar` | The question's number, its dimension, and its pillar |
| `dimension_name`, `topic` | The dimension's name and the question's topic |
| `score` | 0 for A, 1 for B, 2 for C, 3 for D, or NA |
| `note` | The assessor's note, where the answer needs context |

```csv file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/assessment-to-pathway/question_scores.csv
```

1. Enter the answers from the assessment form or its export, one row per
   question, with the assessor's notes where they exist.
2. Run the profile script. A dimension's average is the unweighted mean
   of its applicable questions, a pillar's average the mean of its six
   dimensions, and the overall score the mean of the two pillars scaled
   to 100.
3. Read the level letter as the level nearest the average: A below 0.5,
   B to 1.5, C to 2.5, D above. This is the convention of this guide for
   planning; the framework's own scripts are the reference for the
   published index.

```bash
python readiness_profile.py question_scores.csv
```

**Worked example.** The example organization's profile places ten
dimensions at B and two at A. The "at A" column shows where the A answers
sit: all five questions of agentic AI access, three of the five skills
questions, and two questions in most of the other dimensions. Metadata
standards is the strongest dimension, with an average of 1.40 and no
question at A, because the organization already uses the Metadata Editor
and a standard schema for its microdata. The overall score of 25.6 says
the organization is early-stage, which the framework describes as the
expected starting point.

**What this does not do.** The script computes what the answers say. It
does not judge the answers, and an assessment answered without evidence
produces a profile that the re-assessment of chapter 7 will contradict.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.2 Read each dimension with its questions and notes" level="Foundational" skills="The planning team with the heads of the units assessed" time="Half a day">

**Result:** for each dimension, the planning team can say which questions
hold it at its level and what the assessor's notes say is missing.

A dimension level is an average, and averages hide shape. The framework's
four-step logic calls the second step "diagnose": for each priority
dimension, review the question-level responses and notes to identify the
specific practices, tools, or policies that are missing. This recipe is
that step, done for every dimension before targets are set, so that the
targets of chapter 2 are set with the questions in view.

1. For each dimension, list the questions with their score and note,
   lowest first.
2. For each question at A or B, write one sentence on what the next
   level's response option describes. The framework's reference guide
   gives the four options per question; the sentence is the gap in the
   organization's own words.
3. Mark the questions whose answer the organization disputes or cannot
   evidence, so that the re-assessment starts from an agreed baseline.
4. Keep the list with the score file; it is the first page of the
   pathway document.

**Worked example.** For strategy and governance, the example organization's
two A answers are AI risk management (no risk register for AI components)
and change management (no plan). The three B answers say that AI is
mentioned in the ICT plan without a strategy, that oversight is informal,
and that ethics principles are cited without being adopted. The next
level for each is a document: a risk register with tiers, a change and
training plan, an AI strategy, a board with terms of reference, and
adopted principles. For agentic access, all five answers are A, and the
team notes that the next level for each depends on an API that does not
yet exist, which is what the sequence of chapter 3 makes explicit.

**What this does not do.** Reading the questions says what is missing. It
does not say what the organization should aim for or in what order; those
are the decisions of chapters 2 and 3.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Scoring source.** The framework's assessment platform computes scores automatically through its API; the script of this chapter reproduces the published rule so that a planning team can work from a CSV export or from answers entered by hand.
- **Visualization.** The framework suggests a radar chart of dimension scores. A bar per dimension with the number of questions at each level shows the shape the recipe reads.
- **Versioning.** The score file carries the framework version in its name or a header line, because scores across versions are not comparable.

## Common mistakes

- **Planning from the overall score.** It compresses sixty-nine answers into one number and hides the two dimensions at A.
- **Reading a dimension level without its questions.** Two dimensions at B can need entirely different work.
- **Treating low scores on frontier dimensions as failures.** The framework expects them for most organizations; the sequence puts them last.
- **Losing the assessor's notes.** The notes say why an answer was given, and the re-assessment needs them.

## Verification

- **Reproduction check.** The script's averages equal the assessment platform's dimension scores, where the platform was used.
- **Reading check.** For a dimension picked at random, the planning team can name its lowest questions and the next level's response option for each.
- **Baseline check.** The disputed answers, if any, are marked in the file.
