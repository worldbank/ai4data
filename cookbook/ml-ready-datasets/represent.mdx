# 5. Coverage and representativeness

**Question:** How are coverage and representativeness documented and measured?

## Rationale

A model learns the patterns in its examples and reproduces them. A
coding model trained on job descriptions from the capital codes rural
occupations badly; a model trained mostly on men's jobs codes women's
jobs worse. Whether that matters depends on where the model will be
used, and the person who decides needs to know the composition of the
examples. A statistical organization is well placed to say: it holds
the census and the survey weights, so it can compare the dataset's
composition with the population's, group by group, and state the
difference.

Representativeness is one of the attributes the program names for
training data, and it is the attribute a statistical organization can
document better than anyone else. The point of the measurement is to
inform, and the report of this chapter is written into the card as a
statement of fact: these groups are over-represented, these under, these
have too few examples to learn from, and the label covers these
categories. Whether to reweight, to collect more examples, or to limit
the model's use is a decision the report makes possible.

## Target state

- Every dataset's composition is compared with population benchmarks on the variables that matter for the task, unweighted and weighted, and the differences are stated in the card.
- Groups with too few examples to learn from are listed, and so is the coverage of the label's categories.
- The benchmarks and the thresholds are recorded, and the report is regenerated at each release.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Run the report against census benchmarks on three or four variables; write the result into the card. |
| **AI-ready** | Add cross-tabulations of the groups the task depends on and the coverage of the label; set the minimum count per group; regenerate the report at each release. |
| **AI-native** | Measure a model's error per group on the test split and publish it with the dataset, so that the composition and its consequence are both known. |

:::info[Running example]
The occupation-coding dataset is compared with the 2024 census on
region, urban or rural residence, sex, and age group. It
over-represents the Central Region, urban residents, and men, and
under-represents the Northern Region and persons aged 15 to 24. All
44 occupation codes it contains have fewer than 30 examples.
:::

Each block is one variable, with its benchmark source. The columns give
the count of records in each category, its unweighted share of the
dataset, its weighted share, its share of the population, and the
difference in percentage points, with a flag where the difference
exceeds the stated limit. The summary lines count the thin categories
and the categories that differ.

1. Choose the variables: the ones a model's error could differ by, and
   the ones users will ask about. Region, residence, sex, and age are
   the usual minimum; language and education where the task depends on
   them.
2. Take the benchmarks from the census or the population estimates for
   the same population definition as the dataset (here persons aged 15
   to 64), and record the source and year in the file.
3. Run the report. Read the difference column: the Northern Region
   holds 14 percent of the examples and 27 percent of the population.
   Read the weighted column: the survey weight narrows the gap on
   region and residence (the design under-sampled rural areas) and
   does nothing for the extract's own selection on sex.
4. Write the result into the card's representativeness section, as
   the example card does: the groups that differ, by how much, and
   what the weight corrects.

**Worked example.** The methodologist reads the run above. Urban
residents are two thirds of the examples and two fifths of the
population, a gap of 25 points that the survey weight narrows to 19,
because the design sampled urban areas more heavily on purpose. Men are
64 percent of the examples and 49 percent of the population, and the
weight does nothing about it, because the gap comes from who was
employed and coded, which the weight does not correct. The Northern
Region is 14 percent of the examples against 27 percent of the
population. The representativeness section of the card gains three
sentences stating these gaps and the note "the survey weight corrects
the design's sampling of urban areas and nothing else; a model trained
on these examples has seen few rural and few northern job descriptions".

**What this does not do.** The report compares marginal shares one
variable at a time. Joint gaps (women aged 55 to 64 in the Northern
Region) need the cross-tabulations of recipe 5.2, and the report says
nothing about why the gaps exist, which the collection process section
of the card explains.

<Recipe title="5.2 Report thin groups and the coverage of the label" level="AI-ready" skills="Methodologist" time="Half a day per dataset">

**Result:** the groups, and the combinations of groups, with too few
examples to learn from are listed, and the card states which categories
of the label the dataset covers and how many examples each has.

1. Set the minimum count per group for the task. Thirty examples is a
   floor for a category to be learnable at all; a text classifier
   needs more per label. The report flags groups below the minimum.
2. Cross-tabulate the groups the task depends on (region by sex,
   residence by age group) and list the cells below the minimum.
3. Count the examples per category of the label. In the running
   example, 600 records cover 44 of the 436 ISCO-08 unit groups and
   none has 30 examples; a real dataset for this task has thousands of
   examples and still has rare occupations with a handful. State the
   coverage and the counts in the card, and name the categories a
   model will not learn.
4. Where the gaps matter for the intended use, say what the
   organization did: collected more examples from the next round,
   added synthetic examples (marked as such), or limited the stated
   use.

**Worked example.** The cross-tabulation of region by sex on the 600
records finds two cells below 30: women in the Northern Region, with 28
examples, and women in the Western Region, with 26. The label count
finds that the 600 records cover 44 of the 436 ISCO-08 unit groups and
that the most common, shop sales assistants, has 29 examples. The card states both facts and says that the dataset is an
illustration of the format. It adds that the full dataset of 38,000
records covers 310 unit groups, with 60 of them above 100 examples, and
that a model should not be expected to learn a code with fewer than 30
examples. For the two thin
cells, the card says that the next round's examples will be added in
version 1.1.

**What this does not do.** Thin groups are a fact about the dataset.
Whether a model's error is worse on them is measured on the test split
(recipe 5.3), and the two are published together.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="5.3 Publish the model's error by group with the dataset" level="AI-native" skills="Data scientist; methodologist" time="One day">

**Result:** a reference model trained on the dataset has its error on
the test split measured by group, and the result is published with the
dataset so that users see the consequence of the composition as well as
the composition.

1. Train a simple reference model on the training split and score it
   on the test split, overall and by the groups of recipe 5.1, with
   the measures of the task (accuracy by level for coding, as the
   [production cookbook](/cookbook/language-models-in-production/coding)
   measures it).
2. Report the scores by group with their counts, and read them against
   the representativeness report. A group that is thin and scores low
   is a gap to close; a group that is thin and scores well is a
   warning that the test split is too small to see a difference.
3. Publish the table with the dataset as a baseline, with the model's
   description and the version of the dataset it was trained on.
4. Repeat at each release. A new version that adds examples for a
   thin group should move its score.

**Worked example.** On the full dataset, a simple reference coder that
retrieves index entries and chooses among them scores 0.88 at four
digits on the test part overall. By group it scores 0.90 for urban and
0.81 for rural respondents, 0.89 for men and 0.85 for women, and 0.79
in the Northern Region on 140 test records. The gap between urban and
rural is the one the representativeness report predicted, and the
Northern Region's score is read with care because 140 records give a
wide interval. The table is published with the dataset as the baseline,
with the model described and the dataset version named, and version
1.1, which adds the next round's northern examples, moves the Northern
Region's score to 0.84.

**What this does not do.** A baseline model's error by group describes
that model. A user's model can do better or worse on the same groups;
the baseline tells them where to look.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Benchmarks:** the census; population estimates; the survey's own weighted estimates for the population the dataset was drawn from.
- **Measures:** share differences in percentage points (this chapter); ratios; a summary index such as the total variation distance of the [synthetic data cookbook](/cookbook/synthetic-data-for-sharing/utility).
- **Remedies, stated in the card:** more examples from later rounds; reweighting for training with the reason stated; synthetic examples marked as such; limited stated use.

## Common mistakes

- **"Representative" because the source survey was.** An extract of coded records is selected by who was employed, who answered, and who was coded; the report measures the extract.
- **Survey weights used as training weights without a reason.** They correct population estimates; whether they help a classifier is an empirical question stated in the card if tried.
- **Marginals only.** The gaps that matter are often joint.

## Verification

- **Report.** The report runs at each release against recorded benchmarks, and its numbers appear in the card.
- **Thin groups and label coverage.** Both are listed in the card with the minimum count used.
- **Baseline by group.** The baseline is published with the dataset at the AI-native level.
