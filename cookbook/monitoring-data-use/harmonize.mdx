# 7. Harmonization of mentions

**Question:** Can mentions be matched to the datasets they refer to?

## Rationale

"Household Budget Survey 2021", "HBS", "the budget survey", and "the
household survey of 2021" are one dataset. A count by mention text
spreads that one survey's use over a dozen rows and hides it, so that
the most used dataset in the collection can look like four minor ones.
Harmonization maps each mention to a canonical identifier, with a match
type and a score that say how the match was made and how sure it is,
and it leaves what it cannot match for a person to review.

The review is where the names table of [chapter 1](./define.mdx) grows.
Every variant a reviewer confirms is matched automatically from then
on, so the share of mentions that need a person falls with each run.

## Target state

- Every mention carries a dataset identifier, a match type, and a score, or is marked unmatched.
- Editions and vintages are distinguished where the typology needs it (the 2021 survey from the 2016 one).
- Other organizations' datasets found alongside are kept as co-use, and unmatched mentions are reviewed on a schedule.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Match mentions to the canonical names and acronyms exactly; review the rest by hand. |
| **AI-ready** | Match by exact, phrase, and fuzzy rules against the table with variants. Review unmatched and low-score matches each run and add variants. Track other organizations' data as co-use. |
| **AI-native** | Add semantic matching and clustering (the program's harmonization step) for new variants, distinguish editions, and feed confirmed variants back to the table automatically. |

:::info[Running example]
Of the twelve mentions, string matching resolves ten to the organization's
datasets: eight exactly and two by a phrase inside the mention. The two unmatched mentions are other organizations' data (the
Demographic and Health Survey and the World Development Indicators), which
the report keeps as co-use.
:::

Output for the running example (abridged):

```text
exact      1.00  D01  'Household Budget Survey 2021'                         -> EX-HBS-2021
exact      1.00  D01  'HBS'                                                  -> EX-HBS-2021
contains   0.63  D03  'the labour survey'                                    -> EX-LFS
none       0.00  D04  'Demographic and Health Survey 2016'                   -> -
exact      1.00  D05  'unemployment rate from the LFS'                       -> LF_UNEMP_PCT
none       0.00  D06  'World Development Indicators'                         -> -
12 mentions: exact 8, contains 2, fuzzy 0, none 2
```

Each line shows the match type, the score, the document, the mention as
written, and the identifier it was matched to. An exact match scores
1.00. A "contains" match, where the mention holds a known name inside a
longer phrase, scores by the share of the mention the name covers. A
mention that matches nothing is marked `none` and goes to review.

1. Run the matcher.
2. Read the match types. Exact matches are safe; "contains" and fuzzy
   matches are checked on a sample; unmatched mentions go to recipe
   7.3.
3. For large collections, use the program's harmonization step, which
   adds matching by meaning (sentence embeddings) and clusters mentions
   before matching. Run this script as the baseline, to see what string
   rules alone resolve.

**Worked example.** In the run above, "Household Budget Survey 2021" and
"HBS" match the canonical name and the acronym of `EX-HBS-2021` exactly.
"The labour survey" contains the variant "labour survey", which the
names table lists for the labour force survey, so it matches `EX-LFS` by
containment with a score of 0.63, and the dissemination officer confirms
it on the sample check. "Unemployment rate from the LFS" matches the
indicator `LF_UNEMP_PCT` exactly, because the names table lists that
phrase as a variant. The two mentions that match nothing name the
Demographic and Health Survey and the World Development Indicators,
which belong to other organizations and go to recipe 7.2.

**What this does not do.** String matching resolves known variants. A
new way of naming a dataset is unmatched until a person adds it. The
matching by meaning in the program's pipeline narrows that gap and
still ends in review.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="7.2 Distinguish editions and track other organizations' data" level="AI-ready" skills="Dissemination officer" time="Part of each review">

**Result:** uses are counted at the right level, either the survey
series or one specific round of it, and the other datasets that the
organization's data are used with are recorded as co-use.

1. Decide per dataset whether to count at the series level (the Labour
   Force Survey) or the edition level (the 2021 Household Budget
   Survey). Give both levels identifiers in the names table, with the
   edition as a child of the series.
2. Match a mention with a year to the edition, and a mention without one
   to the series. Report both levels.
3. Keep unmatched mentions of other organizations' datasets as
   **co-use**: which external data the organization's data are used
   with. This informs partnerships and the gap analysis of
   [chapter 9](./act.mdx).
4. When another organization's dataset recurs, add it to a separate
   table with its own canonical name, so that co-use is counted
   consistently.

**Worked example.** The names table gives the labour force survey the
series identifier `EX-LFS` and the rounds the identifiers
`EX-LFS-2024-Q4`, `EX-LFS-2025-Q1`, and `EX-LFS-2025-Q2`, each with
`EX-LFS` as its parent. The mention "the labour survey" in a news
article carries no year and is counted at the series level. A working
paper that writes "the 2025 Q2 round of the Labour Force Survey" is
matched to `EX-LFS-2025-Q2` and counted at both levels. The two
unmatched mentions from recipe 7.1 are recorded as co-use: the
household budget survey was used alongside the Demographic and Health
Survey in one paper, and the unemployment rate alongside the World
Development Indicators in another. Both external datasets are added to
the co-use table with their own canonical names.

**What this does not do.** Editions distinguished by a year in the text
are the easy case. A mention that names the series while the analysis
used one round is resolved only by reading the methods section, which
the review sample does.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="7.3 Review unmatched and low-score matches" level="AI-ready" skills="Dissemination officer" time="One hour per run">

**Result:** every unmatched or uncertain mention has been decided by a
person, and the names table has been extended with the variants that
were confirmed.

1. List the unmatched mentions and the "contains" and fuzzy matches
   below a chosen score. The matcher prints both.
2. For each, decide: a variant of the organization's dataset (add it to
   the table), another organization's dataset (co-use), or a false
   positive from extraction (add the sentence to the labelled sample as
   a negative example).
3. Re-run the matcher. The mentions now match exactly.
4. Keep the decisions with the date and the person. Disagreements
   between reviewers refine the rules.

**Worked example.** The review queue after the run holds the two
unmatched mentions and the one "contains" match. The officer marks the
Demographic and Health Survey and the World Development Indicators as
other organizations' data, confirms "the labour survey" as a variant of
the labour force survey, and adds "labour survey" to the variants
column of `EX-LFS` with the date and her initials. In a later run, a
mention of "the quarterly jobs survey" is unmatched; the officer reads
the sentence, finds that it refers to the labour force survey, and adds
that variant too. After the matcher runs again, both match exactly, and
the next run's queue is shorter.

**What this does not do.** Review resolves the mentions found. It does
not raise the extractor's recall; misses are addressed through the
labelled sample in [chapter 6](./detect.mdx).

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **String rules:** exact, phrase containment, and similarity ratio (difflib in this guide; RapidFuzz in the program's pipeline).
- **Semantic matching:** sentence embeddings and clustering, as in the program's `harmonize_datasets` step, for variants that string rules miss.
- **Identifiers:** series and edition identifiers from the catalog; DOIs where registered.
- **Review:** a queue of unmatched and low-score matches per run, with decisions recorded.

## Common mistakes

- **Matching too eagerly.** A low fuzzy threshold matches "food security report" to the organization's report when the sentence meant any country's; the review sample catches it.
- **Discarding unmatched mentions.** They are the co-use signal and the source of new variants.
- **One level for all datasets.** A quarterly survey and a decennial census need different levels of counting.
- **Review without a record.** The same decision is made again next run.

## Verification

- **Match rate.** The share of mentions matched, by type, is reported per run, and the unmatched mentions have been reviewed.
- **Sample check.** A sample of phrase and fuzzy matches has been confirmed by a person, and the error rate is recorded.
- **Table growth.** The number of variants added per run is tracked, and it falls over time as the table matures.
