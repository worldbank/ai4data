# Practical Guide to Small and Open Models for Statistical Offices

**A cookbook for IT and data science leads in national statistical organizations.** Version 0.1 (working draft), October 2026.

:::note[Working draft]
This guide is a working draft from the AI for Data – Data for AI program. It
collects practical approaches and open standards. It does not
represent official World Bank Group guidance or policy and is not legal
advice; an organization applies it under its own statistical law, data
protection rules, and institutional policies.
Comments and corrections are welcome as
[GitHub issues](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/issues), and the
[contributing](./contributing.md) page explains how to propose a recipe.
:::

The tasks the other cookbooks give to language models are mostly narrow:
code a response, extract a span, embed a query, draft a short field,
explain a rule. Narrow tasks are where small models with the right
context do as well as large ones, and open-weight models that the
organization downloads and runs are where confidential data can be
processed at all. Together they make language model assistance
affordable and lawful for a statistical organization: a server inside
the network, models whose licences were read and whose files were
verified, adapted to the organization's data, compared with larger
models on the organization's own suite, and chosen by the cheapest
model that passes.

This guide is organized around seven questions. The first two are about
choosing: when a small or open model is enough, and which models are
candidates and under which licences. The next three are about making
them work: how they are run, how they are adapted to the organization's
tasks, and how they compare with larger models on the organization's
own suite. The last two are about keeping them: how they are kept secure
and current, and what they cost and how the capacity is sustained. Each
chapter answers one question with recipes, maturity levels, worked
examples, and a checklist.

The recipes build on the program's
[Efficient and Inclusive AI Applications](/docs/inclusive-ai/) work,
which measures model sizes against task accuracy and shows local
deployment, on the [PI-FT toolkit](/pift-toolkit/pipeline) for
fine-tuning embedding models, and on the evaluation and production
practice of the other cookbooks. The [standards page](./standards.md)
lists each and where it applies.

:::tip[The cheapest model that passes the suite]
Every choice in this guide ends at the same rule: the model for a task is
the cheapest one, on hardware the organization controls where the data
require it, that meets the required score on the organization's own
suite, in the organization's languages. Size, openness, and hosting are
means to that rule.
:::

## Prerequisites

The recipes assume the following are in place or can be arranged:

- At least one narrow task that uses or could use a language model (coding, extraction, embeddings, drafting) with its question set from the evaluation suites cookbook.
- A data scientist or developer with Python and a machine with a GPU or a modern CPU, and someone who can read a licence.
- For the AI-ready level, a server inside the production network and a person to run it; for the AI-native level, a shared service and a budget line.

## First steps

For a team with limited time, this sequence gives the most useful result first:

1. Describe the tasks and apply the rule of [chapter 1](./when.mdx). This takes two hours, and it says which tasks start small.
2. Shortlist two candidates for one narrow task, read their licences, and check their cards, following [chapter 2](./candidates.mdx). This takes a day.
3. Run one of them on an existing machine and measure its throughput, following [chapter 3](./serving.mdx), then run the task's suite as [chapter 5](./comparison.mdx) describes. This takes a day.
4. Lock the model files and isolate the server before anything depends on it ([chapter 6](./security.mdx)) (half a day).

Fine-tuning ([chapter 4](./adaptation.mdx)) and the cost and staffing plan ([chapter 7](./cost.mdx)) follow once the first task runs on a small model and passes.

## Self-assessment

The [self-assessment](./start-here.mdx) takes about five minutes and places
an organization's model capacity at a level in each chapter.

## Maturity levels

Each chapter describes three levels. An organization can sit at different
levels in different chapters, and the first level is a complete, useful
state on its own.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | One small open model runs on an existing machine for one narrow task, with its licence read, its files verified, and its score on the suite known. |
| **AI-ready** | A sized and isolated server serves the organization's narrow tasks with versioned prompts and adaptations, every candidate compared on the suite and the frontier, updates gated, and costs measured. |
| **AI-native** | A shared model service with a registry, routing by the frontier, retraining from recorded decisions, and infrastructure and assets shared across organizations. |

## Running example

The example organization's ten model-assisted tasks, a model card for
its own fine-tuned coder, a sizing of a 7-billion-parameter model on a
24 GB GPU, six candidates on the coding suite with their costs, and a
lock file verified against a test directory with an altered file. All
figures are invented and the model names are placeholders.

## Scope of the examples

The examples are small on purpose: ten tasks, six candidates, one card,
one lock file, so that a reader can run them in an afternoon and see the
shape of each decision. They are starting points. Each recipe ends with
a **What this does not do** note that says where the example stops and
what the full version adds. The models themselves are whatever the
organization shortlists; the scripts here decide, size, compare, verify,
and cost.
