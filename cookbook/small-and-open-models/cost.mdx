# 7. Cost, skills, and sustainability

**Question:** What do they cost, and how is the capacity sustained?

## Rationale

A local model costs hardware, power, and people; a hosted model costs
per query and a dependency. The comparison is over years and includes
what is easy to forget: the staff time to run the server, the updates,
the suite runs, and the skills that leave when a person does. For most statistical organizations the arithmetic favours local open
models for the narrow, high-volume tasks and hosted or large models for
a few others. It also favours sharing. One GPU server serves every
unit's batch jobs. One team maintains the registry and the suites.
Organizations share test sets and fine-tuned models where licences
allow. The sustainability plan names the people, the budget lines, and
the renewal dates.

## Target state

- The cost of each task is known per query under its chosen model, including staff time, and compared yearly with the alternatives.
- A small team with named roles maintains the registry, the serving, the suites, and the adaptations, with the skills documented and shared.
- Hardware and software renewals, model reviews, and budget lines are planned over three years, and shared infrastructure is used where it exists.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Estimate the three-year cost of the first local deployment against hosted use; name the person who runs it. |
| **AI-ready** | Measure cost per query per task in production; staff the roles; plan renewals and reviews; share the server across units. |
| **AI-native** | Share infrastructure, test sets, and fine-tuned models across organizations; report cost and capacity yearly with the AI component register. |

:::info[Running example]
The example organization runs its coding, extraction, and embedding
tasks on one 24 GB GPU server inside the production network and uses a
hosted model for release commentary and the public assistant. Its
three-year estimate puts the server, power, and a half-time engineer
against the hosted alternative for the same volumes; the local tasks
cost a fraction per query, and the hosted tasks stay hosted because
their volume is low and their quality requirement high.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="7.1 Estimate the total cost over three years" level="Foundational" skills="Head of IT; finance" time="Half a day">

**Result:** the organization has a comparison of what it would cost
over three years to run its tasks on a local server against using a
provider's service for the same tasks and volumes, with every
assumption written down.

A local model costs hardware, electricity, and the time of the person
who runs it. A hosted model costs a price per request and creates a
dependency. Neither is cheaper in general; the arithmetic for the
organization's own tasks and volumes decides, and the tasks that cannot
use a hosted model at all (confidential inputs) belong in it.

1. Cost the local option: the server from [chapter 3](./serving.mdx),
   spread over three years; power and hosting; the share of an
   engineer's time for running and updating it; and the time of the
   evaluation runs.
2. Cost the hosted option: the measured tokens per request times the
   provider's prices, times each task's volume; the staff time for
   integration and monitoring; and the cost of the tasks that could not
   be sent to a provider and would stay manual.
3. Compare task by task and in total. The frontier of
   [chapter 5](./comparison.mdx) gives the cost per request for each
   candidate.
4. Write the assumptions down with the estimate: volumes, prices,
   salaries, hardware lifetime. Revisit them every year.

**Worked example.** The local option is one server with a 24 GB card at 9,000, spread
over three years, plus 600 a year in power and hosting. It also costs
half of an engineer's time at 18,000 a year and about 40 hours a year
of suite runs. That comes to roughly 65,000 over three years. The hosted option covers the same volumes, which are 250,000 coding
responses, 100,000 documents for mention extraction, and 500,000
embeddings a month. It comes to about 48,000 over three years in token
costs at current prices, plus 9,000 of integration and monitoring time.
It cannot take the coding task at all, because the responses are
confidential, so that task would stay manual at an estimated 90,000 in
coder time. Task by task, the local
option wins on the three high-volume tasks, and the hosted option stays
for the commentary and the assistant, whose volumes are small. The
assumptions are listed on the second page of the estimate with the
date.

**What this does not do.** An estimate with written assumptions is a
plan. After a year in production the measured cost per request replaces
it, in the way the [production cookbook](/cookbook/language-models-in-production/running)
measures batch jobs.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="7.2 Staff the roles and document the skills" level="AI-ready" skills="Head of the unit" time="Half a day">

**Result:** each role the capacity needs has a named person, the routine
tasks have written procedures, and a second person is trained for each
role.

1. Assign four roles: a model engineer (the registry, the server,
   updates, verification); an evaluation owner (the test suites, the
   comparison rule, the report cards); a task owner for each adapted
   task (its prompt, its data, its decisions); and a security contact.
   One person may hold two roles. No role may be empty.
2. Write the routine procedures down as short runbooks: importing a
   model, verifying and deploying it, running the suite, restoring the
   previous version. The scripts in these cookbooks are the core of each
   runbook.
3. Train a second person for each role. The switch-off test of the
   [dissemination cookbook](/cookbook/ai-ready-dissemination/sustain)
   doubles as a staffing test when the second person runs it.
4. Share the training with other organizations through the program's
   events and materials.

**Worked example.** The example organization's register names the data
scientist as model engineer and the methodologist as evaluation owner,
and the coding supervisor, the metadata lead, and the dissemination
officer as task owners for coding, drafting, and extraction. The
security contact is the head of IT. The runbook for importing a model
is two pages built around the verification script of chapter 6, and
the runbook for running the suite is one page around the scripts of the
evaluation cookbook. A junior developer is trained as the second model
engineer and runs the switch-off test in October from the runbook
alone, which takes her ninety minutes and finds one undocumented step,
now added.

**What this does not do.** Roles and runbooks keep the capacity
running. They do not turn the organization into a research laboratory,
and they should not: the scope stays the narrow tasks that the frontier
favours, and the ambitious tasks use hosted or shared capacity.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="7.3 Share infrastructure, test sets, and models" level="AI-native" skills="Head of IT; head of the unit" time="Ongoing">

**Result:** all of the organization's units share one serving capacity,
and test sets and fine-tuned models are shared with other organizations
where licences and data allow.

1. Inside the organization, run one model service with a quota for
   each unit ([chapter 3](./serving.mdx)), one registry, and one
   evaluation owner, and allocate the cost by measured use.
2. Across organizations, publish the question sets whose content is
   public ([evaluation cookbook, chapter 9](/cookbook/evaluation-suites/reporting));
   share fine-tuned models whose training data allow it, with their
   cards; and use pooled infrastructure where a regional or program
   arrangement exists.
3. Record what is shared, and under which terms, in the AI component
   register.

**Worked example.** The labour statistics, metadata, and dissemination
units all call the one server through the gateway, each with a quota,
and the cost is allocated by the measured calls at the end of each
quarter. The organization publishes its catalog question set under an
open licence through the program, and a neighbouring organization that
codes to the same classification asks for the fine-tuned coder. Its
training data were the organization's own confidential responses, so
the model cannot be shared, but the prompt, the index format, and the
training recipe can, and they are. The register records the shared
question set, the shared recipe, and the terms of each.

**What this does not do.** Sharing lowers cost and raises quality for
tasks that many organizations have in common, such as coding to an
international classification or catalog search. Tasks on national data
and in the national language remain each organization's own to adapt.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Cost model:** amortized hardware, power, staff share, suite time against measured hosted spend; per task and in total; assumptions recorded.
- **Roles:** model engineer, evaluation owner, task owners, security contact; runbooks from the cookbooks' scripts; a second person per role.
- **Sharing:** one service and registry per organization; public question sets and shareable fine-tuned models across organizations; pooled infrastructure where available.

## Common mistakes

- **Hardware without the engineer.** The server idles and the models age.
- **Hosted costs without the data that cannot be sent.** Those tasks stay manual, which is a cost.
- **One person who knows everything.** The capacity leaves with them.
- **Every unit with its own server.** One service, quotas, measured use.

## Verification

- **Cost.** A three-year estimate exists with written assumptions and has been revisited within the year, and the cost per query per task is measured in production.
- **Roles.** The roles are named, with runbooks and a trained second person, and the second person has run the switch-off test.
- **Sharing.** The organization has one service and one registry, and the shared assets are recorded with their terms.
