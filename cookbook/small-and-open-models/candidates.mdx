# 2. Candidate models and licences

**Question:** Which models are candidates, and under which licences?

## Rationale

Open-weight models are published by companies, research groups, and
communities under licences that range from permissive (Apache 2.0, MIT)
to open-weight licences with use restrictions and acceptable-use
policies, and some that allow reading the weights and forbid using them. The
differences matter to a public organization: whether the model may be
used for the organization's purposes, modified, redistributed inside
the organization, and run for others. A candidate is a model whose
licence a person has read and found acceptable, whose model card states
what the organization needs to know (languages, training data,
evaluation, limitations), and whose weights can be verified. The
shortlist is small, by design, because every candidate is run through
the suite.

## Target state

- Candidate models are found through a few named sources and shortlisted by language coverage, size class, and licence.
- Every candidate's licence is read and classified (permissive, restricted, or unusable) by a named person, with the restrictions recorded.
- Every candidate has a model card that passes the organization's check, and its weights have a recorded digest.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Shortlist two or three candidates per size class from named sources; read and classify their licences. |
| **AI-ready** | Check each candidate's model card; record digests; keep the shortlist in a registry with the licence classification. |
| **AI-native** | Review the shortlist on each notable release; maintain the organization's own fine-tuned models with their own cards. |

:::info[Running example]
The example organization keeps a shortlist of six candidates for its
coding task: three local open-weight models of 3, 8, and 14 billion
parameters, a large local one, and two hosted ones. Its own fine-tuned
3-billion coder has a model card that passes the check: licence,
languages, training data that never left the organization, an
evaluation with a date and numbers, limitations, hardware, contact.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="2.1 Find candidates from named sources and shortlist" level="Foundational" skills="Data scientist" time="Half a day">

**Result:** each kind of task has a shortlist of two or three candidate
models per size class, with each model's languages and licence noted.

Hundreds of open-weight models are published every year. A shortlist is
needed because every candidate costs a full evaluation run; the
evaluation decides, and the shortlist only limits how many runs there are. Three facts are enough to
shortlist: whether the model was trained or tested on the national
language, whether it fits the hardware the organization has, and whether
its licence can be accepted.

1. Look in three places: model hubs that let models be filtered by
   licence and language (Hugging Face is the largest); the public
   leaderboards for text generation and for embeddings; and the models
   the program has measured on statistical tasks
   ([Efficient and Inclusive AI](/docs/inclusive-ai/)).
2. Keep a model only if the national language appears in its training
   data or its published evaluation, if it fits a size class the hardware
   allows ([chapter 3](./serving.mdx)), if its licence is acceptable
   (recipe 2.2), and if its publisher releases versions and fixes.
3. Keep two or three per size class (small, mid-sized, large). More
   candidates cost more evaluation runs than they return.
4. Record each candidate with its source, version, and the date.

**Worked example.** For the coding task, the data scientist filters the
model hub for text-generation models under permissive or open-weight
licences whose cards list French and Swahili, the organization's
languages, and reads the program's measurements. The shortlist has
three local candidates in the small, mid-sized, and large classes,
one large local model as the ceiling, and two hosted models. One
popular model is left out because its card lists neither language, and
another because its publisher has released nothing for two years. Each
candidate is recorded with its hub address, its version tag, and the
date of the shortlist.

**What this does not do.** A leaderboard score is a result on someone
else's questions. It says which models are worth testing; it does not
say how they will do on the organization's tasks and languages.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="2.2 Read and classify the licence" level="Foundational" skills="Legal; data scientist" time="An hour per candidate">

**Result:** each candidate has a one-line classification of its licence
for the organization's uses, with any restrictions recorded.

An open-weight model comes with a licence text. Some are the same
permissive licences used for open-source software (Apache 2.0, MIT),
which allow use, modification, and redistribution. Others allow use but
restrict it: to organizations below a size, to listed purposes, or under
an acceptable-use policy. A few allow downloading the weights and
forbid using them. A public body needs to know which it has before it
builds anything on the model.

1. Read the licence text and any acceptable-use policy. Note what it
   says about: use by a public body; modification (fine-tuning counts as
   modification); redistribution inside the organization; serving the
   model's outputs to the public; and attribution.
2. Classify the licence as permissive, restricted (with the restrictions
   listed), or unusable for the organization.
3. Record the classification, the restrictions, who read the licence,
   and the date, next to the candidate. A new version of a model can
   change the licence; read it again.
4. For a model the organization fine-tunes, choose a licence for the
   derived weights that the base model's licence allows.

**Worked example.** The legal adviser reads the three local candidates'
licences in an afternoon. The small and the mid-sized model are under
Apache 2.0 and are classified permissive. The 14-billion model is under an open-weight licence with an
acceptable-use policy and a clause that forbids use by organizations
above a number of monthly users, which the organization is far below.
It is classified restricted, with the two clauses recorded and the note
"check again before serving outputs to the public". A fourth model whose licence allows research use only is
classified unusable. The classifications, the reader's name, and the
date go into the registry, and the fine-tuned coder derived from the
small model is given Apache 2.0, which the base licence allows.

**What this does not do.** The classification is the organization's own
reading for its own uses. It is not legal advice to anyone else, and a
new use (serving the model to the public, for example) means checking the
restrictions again.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="2.3 Require a model card that passes the check" level="AI-ready" skills="Python; data scientist" time="Minutes per candidate">

**Result:** every candidate, and every model the organization has
fine-tuned itself, has a model card that states the facts the
organization needs, and a script has checked the card before any
evaluation runs.

A model card is a short document that accompanies a model: who made it,
from what data, under which licence, for which uses, with which known
limits. Publishers write one for the models they release; the
organization writes one for any model it fine-tunes, because it is then
the publisher. The example card below is for the organization's own
coding model. It is a JSON file with one field per fact:

| Field | What it states |
|---|---|
| `model_id`, `version`, `weights_sha256` | Which model this is, which version, and a digest (a fingerprint) of its files, so that the files in use can be verified |
| `license`, `license_url` | The licence and where to read it |
| `languages` | The languages the model was trained or tested on |
| `intended_use`, `out_of_scope_use` | What the model is for and what it must not be used for |
| `training_data` | What the model was trained on, and whether confidential or external data were used |
| `evaluation` | The test suite, the date, and the scores |
| `known_limitations`, `hardware`, `contact` | Known weak spots, what it runs on, whom to ask |

```bash

```

Output for the running example:

```text
example-org/stats-coder-3b 1.2.0: licence Apache-2.0, languages en, fr, sw
  ok      model_id
  ok      version
  ok      weights_sha256
  ok      license
  ok      license_url
  ok      languages
  ok      intended_use
  ok      out_of_scope_use
  ok      training_data
  ok      evaluation
  ok      known_limitations
  ok      hardware
  ok      contact
result: PASS
```

The first line names the model, its licence, and its languages. Each
line that follows confirms one required field, and the last line is
the verdict.

1. For a published model, fill the card from the publisher's own model
   card. Where the publisher does not state a fact, write that it is not
   stated; a card with gaps counts against the candidate.
2. For a model the organization fine-tuned, write the card as the
   publisher. The training data statement says what data were used and
   that they stayed inside the organization.
3. Run the check. A card that fails is completed, or the candidate is
   dropped.

**Worked example.** The card shown above is the organization's own, for
the fine-tuned coder. Its training data field reads "48,000 occupation
responses with coder-assigned ISCO-08 codes from the 2024 and 2025
labour force surveys; all data stayed on the organization's servers; no
external data". Its evaluation field names the coding suite, the date,
and the scores of 0.93 overall and 0.90 on the national language. Its
out-of-scope use says "any task other than ISCO-08 coding of
occupation responses; any confidential text outside the organization's
network". For the hosted large model, the card the organization writes
from the publisher's page has "not stated" in the training data field,
which counts against the candidate in the registry.

**What this does not do.** The check confirms that the card says these
things; whether they are true is a separate question. The scores on a card are reproduced on
the organization's own test questions ([chapter 5](./comparison.mdx))
before they count for anything.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Sources:** model hubs with licence and language metadata; generation and embedding leaderboards; the program's measurements.
- **Licence classes:** permissive; restricted (use-based, threshold-based, behavioural); not usable.
- **Cards:** the Hugging Face model card format (Markdown with YAML metadata) or the JSON of this chapter; the check before the suite.
- **Registry:** a table of candidates with version, digest, licence class, card status, and suite results.

## Common mistakes

- **A licence nobody read.** Restrictions appear at deployment.
- **Shortlists of twenty.** Every candidate costs a suite run.
- **Leaderboards as evaluation.** They shortlist; the suite ranks.
- **A fine-tuned model without a card.** The organization is now the publisher.

## Verification

- **Shortlist.** There are two or three candidates per size class, each with its source, version, and date.
- **Licences.** Each licence is classified by a named reader, with restrictions recorded.
- **Cards.** Each card passes the check, and the digests are recorded.
