# 1. The organization's position on AI

**Question:** What should the organization's position on AI use state?

## Rationale

Every other chapter of this guide refers back to a written position. The
register of chapter 2 lists components against the uses the position
permits. The risk tiers of chapter 3 attach controls that the position
names. The board of chapter 4 approves components against it. The
statement of model use of chapter 7 reports what the position allowed.
Without the position, each of these instruments has to restate the rules,
and they drift apart.

A position is useful in proportion to how specific it is. A sentence such
as "AI will be used responsibly" guides nobody on a Monday morning. A
sentence such as "drafts of metadata may be produced with a model and are
published only after a curator approves them" tells a curator what to do.
The policy this chapter produces is built from sentences of the second
kind. It is short, because the organization's staff have to know it, and it
is approved at the top, because it binds every unit.

The [AI-ready dissemination](/cookbook/ai-ready-dissemination/govern)
cookbook gives a first version of this policy for dissemination alone.
This chapter widens it to production, dissemination, and the everyday use
of assistants by staff, and it adds the instruments that the rest of this
guide maintains.

## Target state

- The organization has one approved policy on AI use that names permitted uses, prohibited uses, controls, roles, and a review cycle.
- The policy says which tasks a model may assist and which decisions stay with people, and every unit can find its own work in it.
- The policy is published, and the date of its adoption is in the decision log.
- The policy is reviewed on a schedule, after any incident, and before each re-assessment.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Write the policy from the template with specific permitted and prohibited uses, name the approver, and set a review date. Decide the task boundary. |
| **AI-ready** | Publish the policy, record its adoption in the decision log, and tie each control to the instrument that shows it is followed (the register, the risk register, the suites, the statement). |
| **AI-native** | Review the policy from the evidence of a year: incidents, review outcomes, acceptance rates, and the re-assessment; publish the revisions with their reasons. |

:::info[Running example]
The example organization adopted its policy on the use of AI in official

## Recipes

<Recipe title="1.1 Write the AI-use policy from the template" level="Foundational" skills="Head of methodology, with the legal adviser; management approves" time="One day to draft; approval time varies">

**Result:** the organization has a one-page policy, approved at the top,
that states what AI may and may not be used for and who decides when a new
use is proposed.

The policy has seven sections, and each one answers a question a member of
staff or an auditor will ask. Scope says which systems and which people
the policy binds. Principles restate the professional responsibility of
the organization in terms of AI: models propose, staff decide;
confidentiality holds in every use; methods are published. Permitted uses
list the tasks a model may assist, each with the review that follows it.
Prohibited uses list what may never be done. Controls name the instruments
that show the policy is followed. Roles say who owns each instrument.
Review says when the policy is reread.

1. Start from the running example's
   [policy](pathname:///cookbook-files/governance-and-risk/ai_use_policy.md)
   and replace the organization, the approver, the dates, and the roles.
   The structure is the one the checker expects.
2. Rewrite the permitted uses from the organization's own work. Each item
   names the task, the model's role, and the person who decides. A use
   that nobody has asked for is left out; it can be added by the board
   later.
3. Keep the prohibited uses absolute. "Confidential data are not sent to
   external services" is a rule. "Confidential data are sent to external
   services only when necessary" is not.
4. In the controls section, name the instruments of this guide: the
   register (chapter 2), the risk tiers (chapter 3), the board and the
   decision log (chapter 4), the evaluation suites, and the statement of
   model use (chapter 7). A control that is not tied to an instrument
   cannot be shown to work.
5. Run the checker. It verifies the seven sections, the lists, the
   approver, the review date, and that no template placeholder remains.

```bash
python check_policy.py ai_use_policy.md
```

```text
  ok      Scope
  ok      Principles
  ok      Permitted uses
  ok      Prohibited uses
  ok      Controls
  ok      Roles
  ok      Review
permitted uses: 7 item(s)
prohibited uses: 5 item(s)
approver: Director General
review date: 2026-09-15
placeholders left: none
result: PASS
```

**Worked example.** The example organization's policy permits seven uses. Four concern dissemination: drafting metadata for curator review, ranking catalog records for search, answering user questions from published values with every number verified, and classifying and extracting information from published documents. Three concern production: coding open-text responses with a threshold and a re-coded sample, explaining edit failures to editors, and drafting release commentary with every number verified. It prohibits five: sending confidential or unpublished
data to any external service, publishing unverified generated numbers,
publishing generated text without named approval, letting a component
change data directly, and using AI to make decisions about individuals.
The roles table gives the Director General the approval and the chair of the board, and the head of methodology the policy and the registers. The head of dissemination owns the statement of model use, the head of IT the providers and the cost record, and the legal adviser the licences. The checker passes
with no placeholder left.

**What this does not do.** A policy states what is allowed. Whether it is
followed shows in the register, the decision log, and the suites. A policy
with an approver and a review date is the first instrument; the others are
what make it more than a statement of intent.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.2 Decide which tasks a model may assist and which stay with people" level="Foundational" skills="Head of methodology with the heads of the production and dissemination units" time="Half a day">

**Result:** the organization has a short table that names, for each task
where a model is or could be used, the role of the model and the person who
decides, and a list of the tasks that stay with people.

The task boundary is the part of the position that staff use most. A model
can take one of a few roles in a task. It can propose a value or a code
that a person accepts or changes. It can explain something to a person who
then decides. It can draft text that a person verifies and approves. It can
retrieve and rank material for a person or for another system. It does not
decide. The boundary says which role the model has in each task, and it
lists the tasks where the organization wants no model at all.

| Model role | What the person does | Example in the running example |
|---|---|---|
| Propose | Accepts or changes the proposal | Occupation codes above a confidence threshold, with a re-coded sample |
| Explain | Decides the case with the explanation in front of them | Edit failure explanations for editors |
| Draft | Verifies every figure and approves the text | Release commentary from output tables |
| Retrieve and rank | Reads the result, or another system verifies it | Catalog search; the public assistant with every number verified |
| Decide | Never | Decisions about individuals, respondents, or staff; changes to data or estimates |

1. List the tasks in each unit where a model's output enters the work
   today or is proposed. The heads of units know these; a short meeting
   per unit is enough.
2. Assign each task a model role from the table and name the person who
   decides. A task with no person who decides is a prohibited use until
   one is named.
3. List the tasks that stay with people, and say why in one sentence each.
   The reason is usually that the decision concerns an individual, that it
   changes an official figure directly, or that no measurement of the
   model's performance is possible.
4. Put the table in the permitted uses of the policy, one line per task,
   and the second list in the prohibited uses.

**Worked example.** The example organization's boundary has six tasks with
a model role and three that stay with people. The six are the components
of its register: description drafting (draft), semantic search (retrieve
and rank), occupation coding (propose), edit failure explanations
(explain), commentary drafting (draft), and the public assistant (retrieve
and rank, with every number verified). The three that stay with people are
decisions about individual respondents, changes to microdata or estimates,
and the choice of methodology for a statistic. The
[Language Models in Statistical Production](/cookbook/language-models-in-production/map)
cookbook places the production tasks on the GSBPM map in its first chapter
and gives the three rules that the map check enforces.

**What this does not do.** The boundary names roles. Whether a model
performs its role well enough is the question of the evaluation suite for
each task, and the threshold at which a proposal is accepted without review
is a decision of the methodology unit that the production cookbook covers.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="1.3 Publish the position and set the review cycle" level="AI-ready" skills="Head of dissemination; the board's secretary" time="Two hours">

**Result:** the policy is public, its adoption is in the decision log, and
the dates on which it will be reread are known.

Transparency about the use of AI is part of the transparency of methods
that the Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics require. Users of
the statistics should be able to read what the organization allows a model
to do. Publishing the policy also binds the organization to it in a way an
internal document does not. The review cycle keeps the policy in step with
practice: a year of incidents, reviews, and new uses changes what the
policy should say.

1. Publish the policy on the methods or quality section of the website,
   with its version and date.
2. Record the adoption in the decision log (chapter 4) as a decision of
   type `policy`, with the publication as its follow-up.
3. Set three review triggers: the annual date in the policy, any incident
   that reached users, and the period before an AI-readiness
   re-assessment (chapter 8).
4. At each review, read the policy against the evidence of the period:
   the register, the decision log, the review outcomes, and the incidents.
   Change what the evidence contradicts and record the new version.

**Worked example.** The example organization published version 1.0 on its
methods page and recorded the adoption as decision D001 on 2025-09-15, with
the follow-up "publish the policy on the methods page" due 2025-09-30 and
closed. The review date in the policy is 2026-09-15. The incident of
chapter 4, which did not reach users, did not trigger a review; the
governance review of chapter 8, run on 2026-01-15, did, and it recorded two
licence findings as actions for the policy's next reading.

**What this does not do.** Publication makes the policy visible, and the
review cycle keeps it current. Neither shows that staff know it. A short
briefing when the policy is adopted, and the task card of the
[AI assistants](/cookbook/ai-assistants-at-work/tasks) cookbook, are how
the policy reaches the people it binds.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Management system standards.** [ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001) describes an AI management system for organizations that want a certifiable structure. The policy of this chapter is its policy clause; the register, the risk register, and the decision log are its records.
- **Risk frameworks.** The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) organizes AI risk work into govern, map, measure, and manage. This chapter is the govern function; chapter 3 is map and measure.
- **Principles.** The [OECD AI Principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles) and the [UN Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics](https://unstats.un.org/fpos/) are the two sources the principles section draws on. A statistical organization restates the second in terms of AI; the first is what its government may already have adopted.
- **Scope choices.** A policy can cover the whole organization or one function. The whole organization is simpler to maintain, because the register, the board, and the statement then have one scope.

## Common mistakes

- **A policy of principles without uses.** A policy that says what the organization values and not what staff may do is read once and forgotten.
- **Permitted uses copied from a vendor.** The uses come from the organization's own tasks. A use nobody has asked for adds a row to the register that nobody owns.
- **Controls without instruments.** "Outputs are reviewed" is a control only when the register says by whom and the review record shows it happened.
- **A review date that passes.** The review is what turns a year of practice into a better policy. The checker reports the date; the board's schedule has to hold it.

## Verification

- **Structure check.** The checker passes on the adopted policy with no placeholder left.
- **Boundary check.** Every component in the register (chapter 2) matches one permitted use, and every prohibited use names something a person could do.
- **Publication check.** The policy's URL resolves, and decision D001 or its equivalent is in the decision log.
- **Review check.** The next review date is in the future, and the last review's changes are recorded with their reasons.
