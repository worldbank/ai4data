# 4. Assessment of quality

**Question:** Can a model assess metadata quality consistently?

## Rationale

A curator can judge a record in a minute; a catalog has thousands. A model
can score every record on the same dimensions, with the same rubric, and
return the scores as structured data that can be aggregated. The program's metadata-quality work does this on four dimensions.
Completeness asks whether the fields are filled. Semantic alignment asks
whether the name and the definition describe the same thing. Specificity
asks whether the text is precise enough to be useful. Consistency asks
whether terms and formats agree across the record.
The scores are useful when they agree with curators on a sample; without
that calibration they are opinions at scale.

## Target state

- Every record has scores on fixed dimensions, produced with a fixed rubric and structured output.
- The scores are calibrated against curator scores on a sample, per dimension, and the calibration is repeated when the model changes.
- Scores are aggregated into a catalog report each period that drives the work list.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Score the start set with a model on the four dimensions; score the same records by hand; compare. |
| **AI-ready** | Score the catalog with structured output; calibrate on a sample of 50 to 100 records scored by two curators; report by field and dimension. |
| **AI-native** | Re-calibrate on every model change; use the scores to order the work list and to measure the completeness gain after decisions are applied. |

:::info[Running example]
The five records scored by a model and by a curator on the four
dimensions. The model agrees exactly on 13 of 20 scores and within one
point on 19; on consistency it scores half a point more leniently than
the curator, which is flagged for calibration.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="4.1 Score records on the four dimensions" level="Foundational" skills="Curator with access to a model; Python for the batch" time="One hour to set up; seconds per record">

**Result:** every record has four scores from 1 to 5 with a one-line
reason each, and the scores tell the curation unit which records need
work and why.

The four dimensions are completeness (are the fields filled), semantic
alignment (do the name, the definition, and the unit agree with each
other), specificity (is the text precise enough to be useful), and
consistency (does the record agree with related records). A rubric is a
written scale: one sentence per dimension and per score, so that a 3
means the same thing for every record. The model reads the record and
the rubric and returns the scores in a fixed format.

1. Fix the rubric. The program's
   [metadata-quality notebook](/docs/notebooks/metadata-quality-assessment-with-llm)
   implements the assessment with such a rubric and a structured output
   schema.
2. Send each record with the rubric and ask for a score and a reason
   per dimension. The reasons are what curators read when they
   disagree.
3. Keep the scores with the model, the rubric version, and the date. A
   change in any of them starts a new series of scores.
4. Score the start set by hand with the same rubric, for the
   calibration of recipe 4.2.

**Worked example.** The poverty headcount record scores 4 on
completeness, with the reason "all required fields filled except
limitation". It scores 2 on semantic alignment, with the reason "the
name says national poverty line, the definition describes the
international line". It scores 4 on specificity. It scores 3 on
consistency, with the reason "the unit is written as % here and as
percent in the related series". The
semantic alignment reason is what the curator acts on, because it names
a real disagreement between two fields that would send users to the
wrong figure. The five records' scores are stored with the model name,
rubric version 1, and the date, and the curator scores the same five by
hand with the same rubric.

**What this does not do.** Scores describe the record's text. They do
not describe the truth of its content: a definition that is complete,
aligned, specific, and consistent can still be wrong, and subject-matter
review catches that.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="4.2 Calibrate the scores against curators" level="AI-ready" skills="Two curators; Python" time="One day for 50 records">

**Result:** for each dimension, the unit knows how often the model's
score agrees with a curator's and in which direction it errs, so that
the model's scores can be read with the right correction.

The two input files have the same layout: one row per record, with the
record identifier and the four scores, one file from the model and one
from a curator. The script compares them dimension by dimension and
reports the share of exact agreements, the share within one point, and
the mean difference (positive when the model scores higher than the
curator):

```python file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/metadata-curation-with-llms/assess_agreement.py
```

```bash
python assess_agreement.py model_scores.csv curator_scores.csv
```

Output for the running example:

```text
5 records, 4 dimensions

dimension               exact  within 1  mean diff  note
completeness             0.80      1.00      -0.20  
semantic_alignment       0.60      1.00      -0.40  
specificity              0.60      1.00      +0.40  
consistency              0.60      0.80      +0.60  model more lenient

1 dimension(s) miscalibrated at a mean difference of 0.5 or more
Scores are compared with the curators' scores as the reference; the curators' own agreement is reported separately.
```

The table has one row per dimension. "Exact" is the share of records on
which the model and the curator gave the same score, "within 1" the
share on which they differed by at most one point, and "mean diff" the
average difference, positive when the model scores higher. A note marks
a dimension whose mean difference reaches half a point.

1. Read the mean difference per dimension. Here the model scores
   consistency more leniently than the curator, so its consistency
   scores are read with that correction, or the rubric wording for
   consistency is tightened.
2. Have two curators score the sample and report their own agreement.
   The model is compared with a reference only as good as that.
3. Re-run after any change to the model or the rubric.

**Worked example.** In the run above, the model and the curator agree
exactly on four of five completeness scores and on three of five for
each other dimension. On consistency the model scores 0.6 points higher
on average, and in one case two points higher, which is why that
dimension is marked. The curator reads the model's consistency reasons
and finds that it treats a unit written two ways as a minor matter,
where the rubric calls it a 2. The rubric's wording for consistency is
tightened to name that case, and the next run agrees within one point on
every record. A second curator then scores the same five records, and
the two curators agree exactly on 15 of 20 scores, which is the ceiling
against which the model's agreement is read.

**What this does not do.** Five records demonstrate; 50 to 100 records
per calibration are needed, across topics and lengths. Agreement on
scores does not show agreement on reasons, so a sample of reasons is
read as well.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="4.3 Aggregate into a catalog report" level="AI-ready" skills="Python" time="Minutes per period">

**Result:** the unit has a report of the catalog's quality by field,
dimension, topic, and producing unit, and a work list ordered so that the
records most used and least good come first.

1. Aggregate the scores by dimension, by the field named in the reasons
   (missing definition, unit mismatch), by topic, and by producing unit.
2. Join with use (the [monitoring cookbook](/cookbook/monitoring-data-use/)
   counts it), so that the work list puts used, low-scoring records
   first.
3. Publish the report internally each period, with the calibration note
   from recipe 4.2 beside it.
4. After decisions are applied ([chapter 5](./review.mdx)), re-score and
   report the gain.

**Worked example.** The first quarterly report over the whole catalog of
340 indicator records shows a mean completeness score of 3.1 and a mean
semantic alignment score of 3.8. The field most often named in the
reasons is `definition_long`, missing or thin in 140 records, followed
by `limitation` in 95. By producing unit, the labour statistics unit's
records score highest and the agriculture unit's lowest. Joined with use,
the work list puts 22 records first: used in the top quartile and scored
below 3 on at least one dimension. After the quarter's decisions are
applied and the records are re-scored, the mean completeness score of
those 22 rises from 2.4 to 4.1, and the report shows the gain beside the
calibration note.

**What this does not do.** A report orders work; it does not do it. The
drafts of [chapter 3](./draft.mdx) and the reviews of
[chapter 5](./review.mdx) are the work.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Dimensions:** the program's four (completeness, semantic alignment, specificity, consistency); others can be added with a rubric.
- **Structured output:** a JSON schema for the scores and reasons, as in the notebook; validation on return.
- **Calibration:** two curators on a sample, agreement measured; the script in this chapter for model versus curators.
- **Reporting:** aggregation by field, dimension, topic, producer; joined with use.

## Common mistakes

- **Scores without a rubric.** The model's 3 and the curator's 3 mean different things.
- **No calibration.** A systematic bias on one dimension misorders the work list.
- **Reasons ignored.** A score is a number; the reason is what a curator can act on.
- **One scoring run.** Scores change with the model; they are a series with a version.

## Verification

- **Rubric.** The rubric is written per dimension and level, and the model and the curators both use it.
- **Calibration.** Agreement and mean difference per dimension are reported, with the curators' own agreement.
- **Report.** The report is produced each period with the calibration note, and the work list follows it.
