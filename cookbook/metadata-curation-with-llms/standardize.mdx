# 6. Alignment with vocabularies

**Question:** Can free text be aligned with controlled vocabularies?

## Rationale

Keywords written freely by many curators over many years say the same
thing in many ways. "Hunger", "food insecurity", and "undernourishment"
describe one concept, and a user who filters on one of them misses the
records tagged with the other two. Search, filters, and AI systems all
treat the three as different, because nothing tells them otherwise.

A controlled vocabulary gives each concept one preferred label, a list
of the other forms people write, and a web address that identifies it.
Mapping the catalog's free text to the vocabulary makes the catalog
consistent for every system that reads it. Most of the mapping is plain
string matching against the alternates, and it runs in seconds. The
terms that do not match are where a language model can propose a
concept for a curator to confirm, and where the vocabulary grows.

## Target state

- Keywords and topics come from a vocabulary with preferred labels, alternates, and URIs, published as a SKOS concept scheme.
- Free text in the catalog is mapped to the vocabulary, with unmapped terms reviewed each period.
- Labels exist in each supported language, produced and reviewed as [chapter 7](./translate.mdx) describes.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Build a list of preferred terms from the most frequent keywords; curators consult it. |
| **AI-ready** | Publish the vocabulary as SKOS with alternates and URIs; map the catalog's free text to it; review unmapped terms; store mapped terms in the records' `keywords` and `topics` with the vocabulary name and URI. |
| **AI-native** | Let a model propose mappings for unmapped terms with a confidence, confirmed in the board; expose the vocabulary through the catalog; add labels per language through the translation workflow of [chapter 7](./translate.mdx). |

:::info[Running example]
Fourteen free-text keywords across the five records against a vocabulary
of ten concepts. Ten map exactly to a preferred label or an alternate,
two map by similarity, and two ("hunger" and "jobless") are unmapped
until an alternate is added or a model proposes the concept.
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="6.1 Build the vocabulary" level="Foundational" skills="Curator; subject-matter staff" time="Two days for the first version">

**Result:** the catalog has a list of the concepts it uses for keywords
and topics, each with one preferred label, a web address that identifies
it, and the other forms curators have written for it.

A controlled vocabulary turns "poverty rate", "poverty headcount", and
"poverty incidence" into one filter. Its minimum form is a
CSV with one row per concept:

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `preferred_label` | The one form used in filters and on pages |
| `uri` | A stable web address that identifies the concept, under the organization's domain |
| `alternates` | The other forms curators wrote, separated by `;` |

```csv file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/metadata-curation-with-llms/vocabulary.csv
```

1. Start from the keywords already in the catalog, ranked by frequency,
   and from the topic list the catalog uses. Merge synonyms into one
   concept each.
2. Give each concept a preferred label, its alternates, and a URI under
   the organization's domain. Where an international vocabulary covers
   the concept, record its URI as well.
3. Publish the scheme as [SKOS](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/),
   the web standard for such vocabularies; the CSV is its minimum form.
   Statistical classifications use [XKOS](https://ddialliance.org/xkos),
   an extension of SKOS.
4. Record the vocabulary's name and URI in the records' `keywords` and
   `topics` fields, which carry a vocabulary and a URI in the World Bank
   schema.

**Worked example.** The vocabulary of the running example has ten
concepts. The row for undernourishment has the preferred label
"Undernourishment", the URI `https://stats.example.org/vocab/undernourishment`,
and the alternates "prevalence of undernourishment; chronic hunger". Food
insecurity and malnutrition have rows of their own, because an alternate
is a synonym of the preferred label and these are related but distinct
measures. Undernourishment is insufficient dietary energy (SDG indicator
2.1.1). Food insecurity is limited access to food (SDG indicator 2.1.2).
Malnutrition covers outcomes such as stunting and wasting (SDG target
2.2). The row for unemployment has the alternates
"unemployment rate; unemployment rates; joblessness". The curator
building it started from the 140 distinct keywords in the catalog,
ranked by frequency, and merged them into ten concepts in a morning,
recording the SDG indicator URI beside the undernourishment concept
because SDG 2.1.1 covers it. The CSV is converted to a SKOS file and
published at the vocabulary's address.

**What this does not do.** A vocabulary built from existing keywords
reflects what curators wrote, which may omit what users search for. The
search logs and the question sets of the dissemination cookbook show
the user side.

<Recipe title="6.2 Map free text to the vocabulary" level="AI-ready" skills="Python; curator for the review" time="Minutes to run; an hour per period to review">

**Result:** every free-text keyword in the catalog is either mapped to a
preferred label of the vocabulary or listed as unmapped for a curator to
decide.

The input is a file of the keywords as curators wrote them, one row per
record and keyword (`record_id`, `keyword`). The script matches each
against the vocabulary's preferred labels and alternates, first exactly
and then by string similarity, and lists what it could not match:

```python file=../../website/static/cookbook-files/metadata-curation-with-llms/map_vocabulary.py
```

```bash
python map_vocabulary.py keywords_freetext.csv vocabulary.csv
```

Output for the running example (abridged):

```text
exact   1.00  FS_UNDERNOURISH_PCT     'food insecurity'                  -> Food insecurity
fuzzy   0.92  LF_UNEMP_PCT            'unemployment rates'               -> Unemployment
none    0.00  FS_UNDERNOURISH_PCT     'hunger'                           -> -
14 keywords: exact 10, fuzzy 2, none 2
unmapped (add an alternate, add a concept, or let a model propose a mapping for review):
  FS_UNDERNOURISH_PCT: 'hunger'
  LF_UNEMP_PCT: 'jobless'
```

Each line shows the match type, the similarity score, the record, the
keyword as the curator wrote it, and the preferred label it was mapped
to. An exact match scores 1.00, a fuzzy match scores by string
similarity, and an unmapped keyword scores zero and appears again in the
list at the end.

1. Accept exact matches. Check fuzzy matches on a sample; a similarity
   threshold of 0.85 catches plurals and spelling, and some false
   friends.
2. For each unmapped term, decide: add it as an alternate of an existing
   concept ("hunger" under Undernourishment), add a new concept, or
   drop it. A language model given the term, its record, and the
   vocabulary can propose the concept with a confidence, for the curator
   to confirm in the board.
3. Write the mapped terms into the records with the vocabulary and URI,
   through the editor.

**Worked example.** In the run above, "food insecurity" maps exactly to
Food insecurity, a concept of its own beside Undernourishment, and
"unemployment rates" maps to Unemployment by similarity with a score of
0.92, because the alternate is the singular. The curator checks the two
fuzzy matches and confirms both. For the two unmapped terms, the model
is given each term, its record, and the vocabulary, and it proposes
Undernourishment for "hunger" with a confidence of 0.9 and Unemployment
for "jobless" with 0.95. The curator confirms both in the board, and
"hunger" and "jobless" are added as alternates. The next run maps all
fourteen keywords exactly, and the records' `keywords` fields now carry
the preferred labels with the vocabulary name and URI.

**What this does not do.** String matching maps known forms. Matching by
meaning (with embeddings) maps more, and a model proposes the rest.
Both end in a curator's confirmation, because a wrong mapping is a wrong
filter for every user.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Vocabulary format:** SKOS for concepts and labels, XKOS for classifications; a CSV with preferred label, URI, and alternates as the working form.
- **Mapping:** exact and fuzzy string matching (this chapter); embeddings for semantic matching; a model for proposals with a confidence.
- **Storage:** `keywords` and `topics` with vocabulary and URI in the World Bank schemas; the Metadata Editor's controlled lists.
- **International vocabularies:** the SDG indicator framework, the classifications of chapter 4 of the microdata cookbook, and thematic thesauri, as sources of URIs.

## World Bank examples

- [Generative AI for Metadata Quality](/docs/metadata-quality/generative-ai-for-metadata-quality) describes controlled vocabulary standardization as a model task: mapping free-text topic keywords to a vocabulary with a confidence.
- The World Bank schemas carry `keywords` and `topics` with `vocabulary` and `uri` per entry.

## Common mistakes

- **A vocabulary nobody uses.** The mapping has to write back into the records, or the list is decoration.
- **Fuzzy matches accepted blind.** "Employment" and "unemployment" are close strings and opposite concepts.
- **Dropping unmapped terms.** They are where the vocabulary learns what curators and users say.

## Verification

- **Vocabulary.** Every concept has a preferred label, a URI, and alternates, and the scheme is published as SKOS.
- **Mapping.** The share of keywords mapped exactly, by similarity, and unmapped is reported each period, and the unmapped terms have been decided.
- **Records.** The keywords and topics carry the vocabulary and the URI.
- **Languages.** Labels exist per language with their review status, from chapter 7.
