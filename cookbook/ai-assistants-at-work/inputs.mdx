# 2. What may be entered into an assistant

**Question:** What may be entered into an assistant?

## Rationale

The most common breach of an AI-use policy is a paste. A note that
contains a respondent's record, an embargoed figure, or a colleague's
phone number is pasted into an assistant to be summarized, and the text
has left the organization before anyone thinks about it. The person meant
no harm; the note was in front of them and the assistant was open. The
rule that prevents it has to be as quick as the paste.

The rule is a tier. Public text, which the organization has published,
may go to any assistant the organization allows. Internal text, which
includes drafts, embargoed material, and anything marked for the
organization only, may go to the organization's own assistant and to no
external service. Confidential text, which includes anything at the level
of a respondent, a household, a business, or a member of staff, and any
identifier, may go only to a local assistant on the organization's own
server, and only for the tasks the card allows. The screening script of
this chapter finds the markers that decide the tier, so that the decision
takes a second.

## Target state

- Every member of staff knows the three tiers and can place a text in one before pasting.
- Internal text goes only to the organization's own assistant; confidential text only to the local assistant, and only for the tasks the card allows.
- A screening script is available where staff paste, and the policy says that a confidential text is not pasted at all until the records and identifiers are removed.
- The organization runs a local assistant for the confidential tasks it allows, or allows none.

## Maturity levels

| Level | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | Teach the three tiers with the card; make the screening script available. |
| **AI-ready** | Run the organization's own assistant for internal text and a local assistant for confidential tasks; log the tier of every use. |
| **AI-native** | Screen inputs automatically at the organization's assistant, and block confidential markers before the text reaches a model. |

:::info[Running example]
An analyst of the example organization wanted an assistant to summarize a
unit note for the Friday meeting. The note carried an embargo line, the
provisional unemployment figures for the second quarter of 2025, a
respondent's national identity number, a phone number, and an email
address. The screening script placed it in the confidential tier and the
analyst used the local assistant on a version with the records removed.
The file is
[draft_note.txt](pathname:///cookbook-files/ai-assistants-at-work/draft_note.txt).
:::

## Recipes

<Recipe title="2.1 Place the text in a tier before pasting" level="Foundational" skills="Every member of staff" time="A few seconds per paste">

**Result:** the person knows, before the text leaves the desk, which tier
it is in and which assistant, if any, may receive it.

The three tiers are the AI-use policy's data classification applied to a
paste. The test is a question each: has the organization published this?
Is it marked as a draft, embargoed, or for the organization only? Does it
contain anything about a person, a household, a business, or a record,
or any identifier?

| Tier | What it contains | Where it may go |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Text the organization has published, in the form it was published | Any assistant the organization allows |
| Internal | Drafts, embargoed figures, meeting notes, internal procedures, unpublished tables | The organization's own assistant only |
| Confidential | Anything at the level of a respondent, household, business, or member of staff; identifiers, contact details, record values | The local assistant only, for the tasks the card allows, and after the identifiers are removed where the task permits |

1. Ask the three questions in order. The first "yes" to the confidential
   question decides; a text with any confidential element is confidential
   as a whole.
2. For a confidential text, remove the records and identifiers if the
   task can be done without them, and screen again. A summary of a note
   rarely needs the identifiers that make it confidential.
3. For an internal text, use the organization's own assistant. If the
   organization has none, the task waits or is done without an assistant.
4. For a public text, use the assistant the card names for the task.

**Worked example.** The analyst's note was for the organization only, so
it was at least internal, and it contained a national identity number, a
phone number, and an email address, so it was confidential. The task, a
summary for a meeting, did not need the identifiers. The analyst removed
the two follow-up items that carried them, which left an internal text
with the embargoed figures, and used the organization's own assistant on
that version. Nothing left the organization.

**What this does not do.** The tiers decide where a text may go. Whether
the task may be done with an assistant at all is the card of chapter 1,
and a confidential task the card does not allow is not done with an
assistant on any server.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="2.2 Screen the text for the markers that decide the tier" level="Foundational" skills="Every member of staff; a developer puts the script where staff paste" time="A few seconds per text">

**Result:** the markers that make a text confidential or internal are
listed with their line numbers, and the text's tier and the advice that
follows from it are printed.

The script looks for the markers that decide the tier. Confidential
markers are identifiers and record references: a national identity
number pattern, a household or respondent reference, a phone number, an
email address, a name attached to a record. Internal markers are the
words the organization uses for unpublished material: embargo,
provisional, unpublished, draft, restricted. A confidential marker makes
the text confidential; an internal marker alone makes it internal; a text
with neither is public, provided the person has asked the first question
of recipe 2.1.

1. Run the script on the text before pasting it. It exits with a failure
   when the text is confidential, so that a habit or a tool can stop the
   paste.
2. Read the lines it names. Where the task can be done without them,
   remove them and run the script again.
3. Add the organization's own identifier patterns and internal words to
   the script; the ones it ships with are the running example's.

```bash
python screen_input.py draft_note.txt
```

```text
confidential markers:
  line 13: national identity number: 4471-20-88193
  line 13: household or respondent reference: Household 00231
  line 13: household or respondent reference: respondent 2
  line 15: phone number: +1 555 0147
  line 16: email address: m.okafor@stats.example
internal markers:
  line 3: embargo: EMBARGOED
  line 4: unpublished or provisional: provisional
  line 4: unpublished or provisional: before the release
  line 8: draft: draft
  line 9: draft: draft
  line 4: internal or restricted: must not leave
tier: confidential
advice: do not enter this text into any assistant outside the organization; use the local assistant, or remove the records and identifiers first
```

**Worked example.** The script found five confidential markers in the
analyst's note, all in the two follow-up items: a national identity
number, a household and a respondent reference, a phone number, and an
email address. It found six internal markers: the embargo line, the
words provisional and "before the release", two mentions of a draft, and
"must not leave". The tier was confidential. With the two follow-up items
removed, a second run found no confidential marker and the same internal
ones, and placed the text in the internal tier, which the organization's
own assistant may receive.

**What this does not do.** The script finds the markers it knows. A text
that describes a person in words without an identifier is still
confidential, and the person who pastes it is the one who knows that;
the script is the second check, and recipe 2.1 is the first.

</Recipe>

<Recipe title="2.3 Run a local assistant for the confidential tasks the card allows" level="AI-ready" skills="IT lead with a data scientist" time="Two to five days to set up">

**Result:** the organization has an assistant on its own server, with no
data leaving the organization, for the tasks the card allows on
confidential text.

Some everyday tasks are worth doing with an assistant and cannot be done
on public or internal text: explaining why a record failed an edit rule,
proposing a variable label from the wording of a question. These need a
model that runs where the data are. An open-weight model on the
organization's own server, with a chat interface that staff reach on the
internal network, is that assistant. The [Small and Open Models](/cookbook/small-and-open-models/serving)
cookbook sizes and runs the server; this recipe says what the everyday
use of it needs.

1. Run an open-weight model on the organization's server, behind the
   internal network, with no connection to an external service.
2. Put a chat interface on it that staff reach with their usual login,
   and mark it as the local assistant on every card.
3. Register the local assistant as a component in the register of the
   governance cookbook, with the card as its human review and the use
   log as its measure.
4. Keep the confidential tasks on the card to the ones the local
   assistant is for, and say on the interface itself which tasks they
   are.

**Worked example.** The example organization runs an open-weight 8B model
on its GPU server for its production components and put a chat interface
on it for staff, reached from the internal network with the usual login.
The card names it the local assistant for two tasks: edit failure
explanations for methodologists and variable labels for curators. The
interface's start page shows the two tasks and the three tiers. The
assistant is row C07 of the component register, with the card as its
review and the use log of chapter 5 as its measure.

**What this does not do.** A local assistant keeps the text inside the
organization. It does not make a confidential task allowed; the card
does that, and a task that is not on the card is not done on the local
assistant either.

</Recipe>

## Implementation options

- **Where the script runs.** As a command for staff who use a terminal; as a check inside the organization's own assistant, run on the pasted text before it reaches the model; as a browser extension for the hosted assistant the organization allows.
- **Identifier patterns.** The national identity number pattern, the phone format, and the internal words differ per organization and are the first thing to edit in the script.
- **The organization's own assistant.** A hosted model under a contract that excludes retention and training, reached through the organization's own interface, serves the internal tier; the [governance cookbook](/cookbook/governance-and-risk/providers) covers the contract terms.

## Common mistakes

- **Pasting the whole note.** The summary rarely needs the identifiers; removing two lines turns a confidential text into an internal one.
- **Treating embargoed figures as public because they will be published.** They are internal until the release time.
- **A local assistant without a card.** It keeps the text inside and allows every task, which the card does not.
- **Patterns left as shipped.** The script knows the running example's identifiers until the organization's own are added.

## Verification

- **Tier check.** Five members of staff picked at random place a sample text in the same tier.
- **Script check.** The script finds the planted markers in the running example's note and the organization's own identifier pattern in a test text.
- **Flow check.** The local assistant has no route to an external service, and the organization's own assistant's contract excludes retention and training.
