# Practical Guide to AI Assistants in Everyday Statistical Work

**A cookbook for all staff of national statistical organizations, and the managers who set the rules for assistant use.** Version 0.1 (working draft), October 2026.

:::note[Working draft]
This guide is a working draft from the AI for Data – Data for AI program. It
collects practical approaches and open standards. Comments and corrections
are welcome as [GitHub issues](https://github.com/worldbank/ai4data/issues),
and the [contributing](./contributing.md) page explains how to propose a
recipe.
:::

The other cookbooks of this series are written for the people who build
and run AI components: developers, curators, methodologists, dissemination
teams. This one is written for everyone else, and for those people when
they are not building anything: the member of staff with a general AI
assistant open in a browser tab, a note to summarize, a table to turn into
a paragraph, an email to shorten, a script to review. These uses are the
most common use of AI in a statistical organization and the least
governed, and the AI-use policy covers them in a line. This guide is what
that line means at a desk.

It is organized around six questions a member of staff asks. Which tasks
may an assistant help with, and which stay with me? What may I paste into
it? How do I ask so that I can check the answer? How do I check the
answer before I use it? How do I record that I used it? And, for the head
of a unit, how does the unit adopt assistants together? Each question has
a small instrument: a one-page card per role, a screening script for the
text before the paste, a prompt library with the source, the format, and
the constraints written in, a script that checks every figure against the
source, a use log and its summary, and a two-hour session that puts them
together on the unit's own texts.

The guide follows the organization's AI-use policy and the data tiers it
sets, and it ties everyday use to the governance of the
[governance cookbook](/cookbook/governance-and-risk/): everyday assistants
are one row of the component register, the card is their review, and the
log is their measure. The [standards page](./standards.md) lists the
sources each chapter builds on.

:::tip[The habit this guide teaches]
Before the paste, the tier. Before the question, the source. After the
answer, the check. After the use, the row. Four habits of a few seconds
each, which together let a statistical organization use assistants for
everyday work without a figure it cannot vouch for leaving a desk.
:::

## Prerequisites

The recipes assume the following are in place or can be arranged:

- An AI-use policy with the three data tiers (public, internal, confidential) and an owner, from the [governance cookbook](/cookbook/governance-and-risk/position).
- At least one assistant the organization allows: its own assistant for internal text, under a contract or on its own server, and, for the confidential tasks it allows, a local assistant on its own server.
- For the AI-ready level, a component register and a decision log where everyday assistants are registered and tier breaches are handled.

## First steps

For a team with limited time, this sequence gives the most useful result first:

1. Agree the task list with the heads of units and print the cards, following [chapter 1](./tasks.mdx). This takes half a day.
2. Put the screening script and the three tiers in front of staff, following [chapter 2](./inputs.mdx). This takes an hour.
3. Hold the first unit session with the card, the script, one library prompt, and the number check, following [chapter 6](./unit.mdx) with [chapters 3](./asking.mdx) and [4](./checking.mdx). This takes two hours per unit.

The use log of [chapter 5](./record.mdx) starts at the first session, and its first summary a month later is where the card and the library are improved.

## Self-assessment

The [self-assessment](./start-here.mdx) takes about five minutes and places
a unit's practice with assistants at a level in each chapter.

## Maturity levels

Each chapter describes three levels. An organization can sit at different
levels in different chapters, and the first level is a complete, useful
state on its own.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Foundational** | The card, the tiers, the three-part question, the check, and the log exist and have been taught once. |
| **AI-ready** | The organization runs its own assistant and a local one, the library and the log are kept and summarized, and everyday assistants are a registered component. |
| **AI-native** | The organization's own assistant serves the library, screens inputs, checks figures, and writes the log, so that the habits are built into the tool. |

## Running example

An analyst in the labour statistics unit of the example organization
wants a summary of a unit note and of the second-quarter 2025 labour
market table for a Friday briefing. The note carries an embargo, the
provisional unemployment figures, and a respondent's identifiers, and
the assistant's summary of the table carries one wrong figure and one
computed difference. The guide follows the analyst through the card, the
screening, the library's question, the number check, and the log row,
and follows the unit through its session and the first quarter's
collection. The figures of the table are those of the shared example of
this series; the note, the answer, and the log are invented.

## Scope of the examples

The examples are small on purpose: one note, one table, five prompts,
twenty log rows, so that a reader can run them in an afternoon and see
the shape of each check. They are starting points. Each recipe ends with
a **What this does not do** note that says where the example stops and
what the full version adds. The scripts find markers, fill templates,
compare figures, and count rows; the judgments, which task, which tier,
which claim, are the person's.
